SUMMARY

Despite the numerous sequencing methods available, the diversity in RNA size and chemical modification makes it difficult to capture all RNAs in a cell. We developed a method that combines quasi-random priming with template switching to construct sequencing libraries from RNA molecules of any length and with any type of 3’ modifications, allowing the sequencing of virtually all RNA species. Our ligation-independent detection of all types of RNA (LIDAR) is a simple, effective tool to identify and quantify all classes of coding and non-coding RNAs. With LIDAR, we comprehensively characterized the transcriptomes of mouse embryonic stem cells, neural progenitor cells, mouse tissues, and sperm. LIDAR detected a much larger variety of tRNA-derived RNAs (tDRs) compared to traditional ligation-dependent sequencing methods, and uncovered tDRs with blocked 3’ ends that had previously escaped detection. Therefore, LIDAR can capture all RNAs in a sample and uncover RNA species with potential regulatory functions.

INTRODUCTION

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells synthesize a vast range of RNA classes that greatly vary in size and function. A criterion for RNA classification is their coding capacity, which distinguishes protein-coding RNAs (messenger RNAs; mRNAs) from non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). The latter can be subdivided into structural ncRNAs, such as ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and transfer RNAs (tRNAs); small nuclear (snRNAs) and nucleolar (snoRNAs) RNAs that participate in splicing and rRNA biogenesis; and regulatory ncRNAs such as PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)1–3. Most regulatory ncRNAs are shorter than 50 nts and often referred to as “small RNAs”4. Regulatory ncRNAs larger than 500 nts are referred to as long ncRNAs (lncRNAs)5.

Next-generation sequencing of RNA (RNA-seq) is a powerful approach to detect and quantify RNAs at the transcriptome-wide level. Library construction techniques can be broadly classified in three categories: mRNA-seq, Smart-seq, and small RNA-seq. In mRNA-seq, polyadenylated (polyA+) mRNAs are purified, fragmented, converted to cDNA via random-primed reverse transcription (RT), and ligated to adapters for amplification and sequencing6. These protocols have been standardized for relatively abundant (> 100 ng) starting material and have been expanded to non-polyA transcripts, by removing rRNAs and skipping the oligo(dT) purification7,8. Smart-seq is based on template switching and generates high-complexity libraries from limiting amounts of mRNA, including from single cells9–13. Neither mRNA-seq nor Smart-seq capture small RNAs, which are typically sequenced by direct ligation of adapters to the 5’ and 3’ termini, followed by cDNA synthesis via RT14,15. While this methodology has been successful, it only clones RNAs with defined chemical structures at their termini, because adapter ligation requires a phosphate in 5’ (5’P) and a hydroxyl group in 3’ (3’OH). Some ncRNAs, however, carry chemical modifications at their termini16,17, hampering standard ligation-based methods. Various strategies have been implemented to overcome this limitation18–30, but they require prior knowledge on the chemical termini to be “repaired”. Thus, an unbiased, ligation-independent method for small RNAs, ideally one that also captures longer RNAs, remains a critical need.

Here, we describe a method to achieve ligation-independent detection of all types of RNA (LIDAR), regardless of size or chemical structure of their 5’ and 3’ termini. We combined a custom template-switching oligo with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and quasi-random hexamer priming to minimize the formation of adapter dimers. This allowed us to skip size selections, maximizing recovery of small and long RNAs. Because LIDAR is ligation-independent, it captures RNA with modifications at their 3’ ends, while retaining sensitivity for low RNA input; therefore, it can be used as an efficient “all-in-one” method to measure all types of RNAs simultaneously. LIDAR captured RNAs from mouse embryonic stem cells (ESCs), tissues, and sperm that are notoriously difficult to clone—including full-length tRNAs and derived fragments and, importantly, revealed the presence of 3’-blocked tRNA-derived RNAs (tDRs) that had previously escaped detection by sequencing.

DESIGN

We developed LIDAR with the goal of extending the ligation-independent nature of Smart-seq towards more RNA classes while retaining its high sensitivity in low-input conditions12. All versions of Smart-seq, including Smart-seq39, rely on template switching to introduce sequences for library amplification at the 5’, bypassing the need to ligate adapters. However, conventional Smart-seq was developed for mRNA and requires a 3’ poly(A). Smart-seq has been modified to poly(A)-RNAs, but still requires in vitro polyadenylation21, which needs a 3’OH, or on random hexamer priming followed by size-selection to remove adapter dimers, thus compromising the detection of small RNAs31.

We introduced four modifications to the Smart-seq3 protocol to clone all types of RNA, regardless of size or 3’ modifications (Fig. 1A). First, we primed the RT with a “quasi-random” hexamer, devoid of cytosine at the 3’ (5’-NNNNND-3’; D = A, T, or G). This reduces annealing to the 3’-rGrGrG of the template-switch oligo (TSO), minimizing adapter dimers (Fig. 1B; S1A–B)32,33. Second, we designed a TSO, with a sparse UMI structure, 5’-NNcgNNagNN-3’, instead of the 5’-NNNNNNNN-3’ used in Smart-seq9. This TSO further reduces the formation of adapter dimers, favoring the generation of libraries with inserts (Fig. 1B; S1B). Third, we omitted the TSO from the first reaction, promoting RT primer annealing to the RNA and reverse transcriptase binding34. RT was performed at 10 minutes at 25 °C (Fig 1A, step I) and the temperature was raised to 42 °C before adding the TSO (Fig. 1A, step II). Fourth, we omitted tagmentation and size-selection after pre-amplification (Fig 1A, step III), proceeding directly to the final PCR (Fig. 1A, step IV). These modifications to Smart-seq3 allowed us to capture RNA of all sizes, including small RNAs.

RESULTS

LIDAR captures small RNAs

Although random hexamers priming is extensively utilized for cDNA synthesis7, it has not been employed to clone small RNAs, likely due to difficulties in separating libraries with small inserts from empty adapter dimers. In fact, the commercial SMARTer total RNA-seq kit, which combines random hexamer priming with template switching in a similar way as the modified Smart-seq3 protocol we tested (Fig. 1B), requires size selection to remove adapter dimers.

LIDAR was designed to suppress adapter dimers without size selection, allowing us to clone small RNAs. We tested LIDAR on 20 and 50 nts RNA oligos ending with 8 random nucleotides. We obtained products of the correct size from as little as 0.6 ng input RNA, indicating good sensitivity (Fig. 1C; S1C). The sequence coverage of both 20 nts (Fig. 1D) and 50 nts (Fig. S1D) RNAs was nearly complete, with 90% of the length covered in > 70% of the reads, indicating that our modified RT-based sequencing could capture small RNAs. In fact, even a ligation-based approach (NEB kit; henceforth referred to as “ligation”) did not result in 100% coverage at the 3’ (Fig. 1D, S1D), likely due to incomplete synthesis or partial degradation of the RNA oligonucleotides.

LIDAR showed no sequence preference in the captured RNAs (Fig. S1E, top), similar to the ligation-based protocol (Fig. S1E, bottom), despite earlier reports of ligation biases especially at the 3’ of the RNA insert35. LIDAR captured 20 nts and 50 nts oligos equally well, whereas the ligation-dependent method preferentially recovered the smaller RNA, consistent with the fact that this protocol was optimized for sequencing small RNAs (Fig. 1E).

Next, we tested LIDAR on total RNA from mouse ESCs. We successfully constructed libraries as little as 1 ng total cellular RNA (Fig. S1F), although with increasing contamination of adapter dimers (Fig. S1G). TSO strand invasion events were limited (Fig. S1H), occurring at a frequency comparable to FLASH-seq, a modification of Smart-Seq developed to reduce these products10. LIDAR libraries contained a substantial number of small inserts (< 100 bp), albeit fewer than in ligation libraries (Fig. S1I). Conversely, library constructed with the commercial SMARTer total RNA-seq kit, contained almost no insert < 100 nts (Fig. S1I).

Because LIDAR was designed to capture all RNAs, we expected a large proportion of reads to map to abundant structural RNAs, such as ribosomal RNA (rRNA). In fact, 64% of LIDAR reads mapped to rRNA, three times more than with the traditional ligation protocol (Fig. S2A), but similar to ligation-based techniques aimed at capturing larger sets of RNAs, such as PANDORA-seq28. Among ncRNAs, LIDAR recovered several short RNA biotypes, such as snoRNA, snRNAs, and tRNAs, while the ligation protocol prevalently captured snoRNAs. SMARTer libraries, on the other hand, contained mostly pseudogenes and scRNAs (Fig. 1F). LIDAR also retrieved mature miRNAs (Fig. 1G), and despite their small size (20–24 nts), their entire length was often covered (Fig. 1G–H). Capture of miRNAs by LIDAR was less efficient than with ligation-based methods optimized for small RNAs, especially for low-abundance species (Fig. S2B). However, we could improve sensititvity for small RNAs by size-selecting the input (Fig. S2C–D) and depleting rRNAs before library construction (Fig. S2A; S2E).

Overall, we show that LIDAR is a versatile, ligation-independent method that allows sequencing of RNAs of any size and biotype from small input amounts in less than 4 hours.

Simultaneous detection of differentially expressed protein-coding and small RNAs

To capture RNA of all sizes, including small RNAs, we omitted the tagmentation step of Smart-seq3, to avoid the generation of unclonable and unmappable fragments. Nonetheless, the quasi-random hexamers should prime RT from internal sites within longer transcripts and, therefore RNAs of all sizes should be detectable in LIDAR libraries. After excluding those mapping to rRNA, a large proportion of the remaining reads originated from mRNAs, which were virtually undetected by the ligation-based method (Fig. S2F). Consistent with this, LIDAR libraries contained inserts from a larger range of original transcript size (Fig. S2G), suggesting that this extensive coverage would allow the detection of differentially expressed transcripts of various biotypes.

We differentiated ESCs into neural progenitor cells (NPCs)—a process accompanied by substantial changes in gene expression36,37 (Fig. 2A). Using conventional mRNA-seq, we detected 8,955 genes differentially expressed (adjusted p < 0.05) between NPCs and ESCs (Fig. 2B, left). These included downregulated pluripotency genes (Sox2, Nanog, Pou5f1) and upregulated neuronal markers (Nestin, Ncam1, Neurog1). LIDAR libraries revealed a comparable number of differentially expressed mRNAs (n = 6,713; adjusted p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B, right), including the same pluriporency and neuronal markers. Comparison of transcript quantification by mRNA-seq or LIDAR showed very similar expression patterns (Fig. 2C), a high degree of overlap (Fig. 2D), and good correlation between fold-changes (Fig. 2E). On the other hand, LIDAR detected fewer miRNAs (Fig. S2H) than ligation-based sequencing, but they were quantified accurately (Fig. 2F–H). LIDAR and ligation-based measurements of snoRNAs were also correlated (Fig. S2I–J), whereas most differentiation-induced changes in snRNAs were only identified by LIDAR (Fig. S2K–L).

These results highlight the effectiveness of LIDAR in analyzing gene expression changes simultaneously in distinct RNA populations, which are typically measured with separate library construction protocols. Nonetheless, for deep analyses of exclusively the coding transcriptome or miRNAs, dedicated methods remain preferable.

LIDAR reveals long 3’ tDRs in ESCs

tRNA-derived RNAs (tDRs)38 also known as tRNA fragments (tRFs) are small ncRNAs with a growing list of functions in the regulation of translation39, transposons40, and transgenerational epigenetics41,42. tDRs are classified according to their size and position within the tRNA sequence (Fig. 3A)43,44. 5’ tDRs start at the 5’ of the mature tRNA and near the D loop (5’ tRNA fragments, or 5’-tRF), or at the anticodon (AC) loop (5’ tRNA halves, or 5’-tiRNAs). 3’ tDRs end at the non-templated 3’-terminal CCA sequence and start at the T-loop (3’-tRF) or at the AC-loop (3’ tRNA halves, or 3’-tiRNAs). tDRs that do not include neither 5’ nor 3’ termini are classified as internal tRNA fragments (i-tRF).

Ligation-based libraries are biased towards the detection of 5’-tRFs and 5’ tRNA halves, and do not capture efficiently 3’ tRFs and 3’ tRNA halves, despite the fact that they can be detected in northern blots27,45. We speculated that chemical modifications at the 3’ end interfere with ligation and wondered whether LIDAR would capture these “unclonable” tDRs. We sequenced size-selected RNAs < 200 nts and detected a far greater proportion of reads mapping to 3’ tDRs compared to ligation-dependent libraries, which showed a bias for 5’ fragments (Fig. 3B), as previously reported27. The size distribution for reads mapping to the 5’ of tRNAs was similar in both methods (Fig. 3C, left), suggesting that LIDAR reads assigned to this class originated from actual 5’ tDRs. On the other hand, the size of 3’ tDRs cloned by LIDAR was markedly different compared to ligation-based libraries (Fig. 3C–D). In LIDAR, the majority of reads mapping to the 3’ of tRNA genes were 30–40 nts, some of them compatible with the size of 3’ tRNA halves, whereas 3’ tDRs captured by ligation were 17–22 nts, corresponding to cleavage events within the T-loop (Fig. 3C, right; Fig. S3A) that generate 3’-tRFs. We call the 30–40 nts tDRs identified by LIDAR as “long” to differentiate them from the shorter 3’-tRFs.

LIDAR libraries from size-selected, < 50 nts RNA inputs contained tDRs with size distribution similar to those observed in the < 200 nts fraction (Fig. 3B–C; S3B), suggesting that the long 3’ tDRs are unlikely to originate from abortive cDNA synthesis from full-length tRNAs. Furthermore, the presence of multiple long 3’ tDRs identified by LIDAR was confirmed by northern blots (Fig. 3D; S3C), although in one case (Ile-TAT-2-1; Fig. S3C) the size appeared smaller than expected. As ligation-based sequencing did not detect fragments derived from this tRNA, we surmise that the 3’ tDRs detected by northern blot corresponds to the long 3’ tDRs identified by LIDAR and that it may carry chemical modifications affecting its migration, such as 2’3’ cyclic phosphate (2’3’-cP;46,47. 3’ tDRs of 30–40 nts have been observed by northern blots across different human tissues and cell lines48, including a 3’ fragment from Arg-TCG-1-149, which we also detected in ESCs (Fig. 3D). This suggests that LIDAR can identify tDRs that escape detection by standard ligation-based methods.

In many cases, LIDAR reads mapping to the 3’ of tRNAs contained the non-templated CCA (Fig. 3D), indicating that they originated from mature tRNAs50. However, in some cases the terminal CCA was incomplete (Fig. 3D), likely due to internal priming. This prompted us to develop a modified LIDAR protocol to increase 3’ end coverage. We designed a random hexamer that protrudes as a 3’ overhang of a double-stranded DNA molecule (Fig. S3D). The double-stranded stretch should decrease internal priming, favoring the initiation of RT at the 3’ end of the RNA. Libraries constructed with this modified protocol (3’-LIDAR) on 20 nts and 50 nts synthetic RNAs, yielded an increased number of reads covering the full sequence, corresponding to increased priming at the 3’ (Fig. S3E). Consistent with this, the size distribution of miRNAs reads in 3’-LIDAR on total ESC RNA more closely resembled the end-to-end coverage observed in ligation libraries (Fig S3F). 3’-LIDAR also improved the coverage of the 3’-terminal non-templated CCA ends of tRNAs (Fig. S3G–H), although at the expense of increased numbers of adapter dimers (Fig. S3I). Size distribution and representation of 3’ tDRs were similar in LIDAR and 3’-LIDAR (Fig. S3J–L), further suggesting that the long, 30–40 nts 3’ tDRs identified in LIDAR are produced in vivo by cleavage of mature, CCA-containing tRNAs.

Long 3’ tDRs are differentially expressed in mouse tissues

We wondered whether 3’ long tDRs could also be detected in vivo, and whether their expression is regulated across tissues, as observed for other types of tDRs49. We constructed LIDAR and ligation-based libraries on RNA from mouse brain and testis. The biotype distributions appeared markedly different, with LIDAR recovering a broader spectrum of ncRNA types in both brain and testis (Fig. S4A). LIDAR reported differences in miRNAs that were in good agreement with those detected by ligation-based sequencing (Fig. 4A–B; S4B). In testis, both methods detected PIWI-associated RNAs52,53 (Fig. 4C; S4A), confirming the ability of LIDAR to clone and measure different classes of small RNAs. A higher fraction of reads mapped to 3’ tDRs in LIDAR compared to the ligation-based libraries (Fig. 4D). Several 30–40 nts 3’ tDRs identified only by LIDAR showed differential expression between brain and testis (Fig. 4E–F), which was confirmed by northern blots (Fig. 4G–H; S4C–F), suggesting tissue-specific regulation of long 3’ tDRs that can be quantified by LIDAR.

LIDAR detects RNA modifications on 3’ tDRs

It is generally believed that most tDRs derive from enzymatic cleavage of mature tRNAs43. Proper tRNAs biogenesis requires extensive chemical modification at stereotypical nucleotide positions51. tRNA modifications often constitute obstacles for reverse transcriptases, resulting in errors during cDNA synthesis that can be utilized to infer the position of modified residues52. Both long 3’ tDRs and shorter 3’-tRFs showed high frequency of mismatches at position 58 in LIDAR (Fig. 5A–B), consistent with the presence of m1A25,51,53, which is typically found on mature tRNAs54. Thus, the 3’ tDRs detected by LIDAR may derive from mature, modified tRNAs. Alternatively, the 3’ tDRs could be directly targeted by the RNA modification machinery. In ligation-based libraries, fewer 3’ tDRs contained mismatches at position 58 (Fig. 5A–B). Since the reverse transcriptases used in the ligation protocol and LIDAR are from the same family (M-MuLV), it is unlikely that the m1A only affects LIDAR, suggesting that another determinant is responsible for the difference in mismatch frequency at this position.

Shi et al. recently described PANDORA-seq, a ligation-based RNA-seq approach that utilizes T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK) to generate 5’P and 3’OH ends, combined with AlkB to remove methyl groups and facilitate RT28. We compared PANDORA-seq data with LIDAR on comparable input material (size-selected RNA from serum-free ESCs) and utilized the same computational strategy to analyze data from both methods. The published PANDORA-seq and our LIDAR reads mapped to a similar but not identical distribution of RNA classes (Fig. S5A). As expected for a ligation-based approach, PANDORA-seq recovered full-length miRNAs at a higher frequency than LIDAR (Fig. S5B). However, although PANDORA-seq detected more 3’ tDRs than conventional ligation, including some long 3’ tDRs, LIDAR was superior in its ability to capture a larger number of both 5’ and 3’ tDRs (Fig. 5C–D; S5C). Furthermore, LIDAR detected 3’ tDR modifications at position 58 (Fig. 5E) that were missed by PANDORA-seq, likely because they were removed by AlkB. Finally, while some PANDORA-seq reads mapped to the same tRNAs that give rise to the long 3’ tDRs identified by LIDAR, the fragment size according to PANDORA-seq was often smaller (Fig. S5D–E) and many long 3’ tDRs went undetected (Fig. S5F).

In conclusion, LIDAR revealed that the 30–40 nts 3’ tDRs identified in ESCs are modified, possibly with m1A, at position 58, and, therefore, are likely cleavage products of mature tRNAs.

LIDAR clones 3’-blocked tRNAs and tDRs

A motivation to develop LIDAR was to allow sequencing of RNAs with 3’ ends blocked by modifications that make them inaccessible to conventional ligation. RNAs with these features include the U6 snRNA55 and tRNAs charged with amino acids (Fig. 6A). Unlike other snRNAs, U6 is transcribed by RNA Pol III and its 3’ terminus is processed to 2’−3’cP56. This 3’ modification, unless resolved by T4 PNK, prevents the ligation of an adapter28. A similar impediment can arise from amino acids which, in a subset of tRNAs, are attached to the 3’OH group50 and can only be removed by chemical de-acylation or β-elimination57,58. Random-hexamer priming should not be affected by the chemical status of the 3’ of the RNA (Fig. 6A) and, indeed, LIDAR cloned a synthetic 3’-blocked RNA that could not be incorporated in ligation-based libraries (Fig. 6B, S6A).

Based on these observations, we reasoned that one cause for RNAs being detected by LIDAR but not ligation may be a 3’ block. Accordingly, LIDAR captured the U6 snRNA, which was virtually absent from ligation-based libraries, likely because of its terminal 2’−3’cP (Fig. S6B). tRNA reads from LIDAR, but not ligation-based cloning, peaked at ~70 nts — the size of mature tRNAs (Fig. 6C). This difference was not due a ligation bias toward smaller inserts, since the larger snoRNAs were efficiently captured by ligation (Fig. S6C). LIDAR reads mapped to tRNAs with a broad range of anticodons, similar to techniques developed specifically to clone mature tRNAs25 (Fig. 6D, left). In contrast, ligation-dependent libraries were biased towards a subset of anticodons (Fig. 6D, right), as reported27. Full-length tRNA reads from LIDAR contained mismatches at sites where mature tRNAs are chemically modified, including position 9, 26, 32, 37, e2, and 58 (Fig. S6D, left), similar to observations made with mim-tRNAseq25. Some of these sites were also detected as mismatches in reads from ligation-based libraries, but at lower frequency (Fig. S6D, right).

Given that more than 80% of mature cytosolic tRNAs are charged57, we conclude that LIDAR captured those species much more efficiently than the ligation-based method, due to its ability to bypass blocked 3’ ends.

To better profile molecules with inaccessible 3’, we treated ESC RNA with PNK to convert 3’P and 2’−3’cP to 3’OH, added a polyA tail, and then removed them with oligo-dT-conjugated beads (Fig. 6E). The resulting supernatant (flow-through) should be enriched for “blocked” RNAs with a 3’ other than -OH, -P, or 2’−3’cP. This RNA fraction was prevalently composed of full-length tRNAs, which appeared as a band of ~70 nts (Fig. 6F). LIDAR reads from blocked RNA mapped to tRNA genes (Fig. S6E) and were enriched for full-length inserts compared to untreated RNA (Fig. S6F–G) and to ligation libraries (Fig. 6G). Other small RNA classes, except a subset of snoRNAs, were captured efficiently in LIDAR libraries from all (i.e. non-blocked) RNA and also detected by ligation (Fig. 6G). We found several 3’ tDRs enriched in the blocked RNA population (Fig. 6H–I). We speculate that some of these 3’ tDRs may be aminoacylated, thus deriving from mature and charged tRNAs. A recent report provided the first evidence of aminoacylated 3’ tDRs59, supporting the existence of a previously inaccessible class of 3’ tDRs that can be detected with LIDAR.

Thus, LIDAR clones RNAs with blocked 3’ and can be used to analyze RNAs not detectable with ligation-based methods, including full-length aminoacylated tRNAs and their 3’ fragments.

LIDAR captures transcript diversity in mouse sperm

Ligation-based small RNA sequencing studies reported that the RNA content of mouse sperm purified from the cauda (distal) region of the epididymis is dominated by small- and medium-size species (< 2 kb), in particular 5’ tDRs41,42. However, analyses of sperm RNA by northern blot or the recently described OTTR-seq27 revealed the presence of 3’ tDRs—and possibly also full-length tRNAs—that were missed by conventional small RNA library preparation protocols45. We tested whether LIDAR could generate a more comprehensive catalog of RNAs in sperm.

RNAs isolated from cauda sperm were small (Fig. S7A), as previously reported27,28,45. We analyzed total sperm RNA with or without enriching for blocked transcripts (see Fig. 6E) with LIDAR or ligation-based libraries as control. After excluding rRNAs, the majority of the RNA fragments cloned from cauda sperm by both LIDAR and ligation originated from tRNAs (Fig. 7A), in line with previous studies41,42,45. The size distribution of cytosolic tRNA reads revealed that a large fraction originated from tDRs rather than full-length mature tRNAs, as previously reported45 (Fig. 7B). On the other hand, reads mapping to mitochondrial tRNAs peaked at ~70 nts in LIDAR libraries and increased after enrichment of blocked RNAs (Fig. 7A–B). This indicates that the mitochondrial tRNAs in sperm are 1) protected from fragmentation and 2) blocked at their 3’ end. This demonstrates that cytosolic tRNA fragments detected by LIDAR are bona fide RNA species, not artifacts.

As we observed in ESCs, 5’ and 3’ tDRs were detected at similar levels by LIDAR, whereas ligation favored 5’ tDRs (Fig. 7C). We extended our comparisons to the published PANDORA-seq dataset on mouse sperm28 and noted a similar underrepresentation of 3’ tDRs compared to LIDAR (Fig. S7B); in particular, PANDORA detected fewer long 3’ tDRs (Fig. S7C–D). The few 3’ tDRs in traditional ligation-based libraries were mostly small (17–22 nts), although larger species were present (Fig. S8A), whereas LIDAR captured mostly fragments 30–40 nts in size (Fig. S8A). Importantly, LIDAR identified several 3’ tDRs not detected or very underrepresented in ligation and PANDORA-seq libraries (Fig. 7D; S7D), with a heavily modified base at position 58 (Fig. S8B), and enriched in the blocked RNA population (Fig. 7D–E; S8C), suggesting that long 3’ tDRs with blocked 3’ ends are also present in sperm.

Many LIDAR reads mapped to rRNA (71.3%) (Fig. S8D), almost three times higher than in ligation libraries (28%), likely because rRNA fragments in sperm have 3’P or 2’3’cP ends that require enzymatic conversion before ligation, as shown by PANDORA-seq28. LIDAR libraries from blocked sperm RNA also contained a large proportion of rRNA reads (70.6%). While it is possible that our biochemical depletion was incomplete, we noticed an enrichment of blocked reads mapping to well-defined regions of 18S and 28S rRNAs (Fig. S8E), suggesting the existence of small rRNA fragments chemically blocked at their 3’ ends. As in the case of ESCs, LIDAR captured a higher amount of U6 (Fig. S8F).

In summary, LIDAR uncovered diversity in the sperm RNA payload, including full length tRNAs and 3’ tDRs that had previously escaped detection.

DISCUSSION

Here, we presented LIDAR, an RNA-seq technique that utilizes a custom template-switch strategy combined with quasi-random hexamer priming to sequence all types of RNA, regardless of size or chemical modifications at 5’ and 3’.

Technical advantages of LIDAR

LIDAR enables quick and comprehensive coding and non-coding transcriptome analysis. The protocol (Fig. 1A) can be completed in less than 4 hours, faster than recent methods based on template switching10,13,21, and with minimal hands-on time. It does not require tagmentation and all molecules contain UMIs, mostly missing in existing small RNA cloning strategies, thus increasing quantification accuracy, especially from low-input samples. A sparse UMI structure allows the sequencing of LIDAR libraries without causing low-complexity issues during cluster detection on Illumina sequencers60,61.

The input RNA for LIDAR does not require chemical or enzymatic pre-processing. This minimizes contaminations and sample loss due to RNA degradation, as observed for some enzymatic treatments16,19. While size-selection of input RNAs by PAGE can mitigate the risk of the latter28, it might also lead to artificial tRNA fragmentation27. LIDAR bypasses the requirement of a priori knowledge of terminal RNA modifications, only some of which can be removed by established methods19,28,30.

The RNA biotype distribution in LIDAR libraries is markedly different from the one obtained by standard ligation. As no method is completely free from biases, it is possible that the two strategies over- and under-represent different RNA species. Comparison of LIDAR with PANDORA-seq showed a much more similar biotype distribution, at least for ncRNAs (Fig. S5A), despite the many differences between the two protocols.

Ligation-based sequencing strategies developed for small RNAs can be used to analyze long RNAs, such as mRNA, but require an enrichment step29. LIDAR can detect transcriptional changes of both long and small RNAs simultaneously from total RNA (Fig. 2; S2). LIDAR identified simultaneously most of the differences in both protein-coding RNAs and small RNAs, detected independently by specialized protocols in ESCs vs. NPCs. Finally, LIDAR requires relatively low input amounts (10–500 ng total RNA), making it amenable for comprehensive transcriptome analyses of biological sources where starting material is limited.

Ligation-independent small RNA sequencing

The ligation of sequencing adapters requires free 3’OH ends on the target RNAs or a means to generate them. For example, PANDORA-seq utilizes enzymatic treatments to generate RNA ends compatible with ligation and to remove internal RNA modification that could stall RT enzymes28. Artificial polyadenylation of small RNAs enables template switching protocols that include an oligo-dT priming step20,21, but only RNAs that contain a 3’OH can be artificially polyadenylated. TGIRT-seq22,23 leverages a group II intron reverse transcriptase to synthesize full-length cDNAs, including from tRNAs24, without the need for 3’ adapter ligation. TGIRT-seq captures small and long RNAs, but suffers from sequence bias, adapter dimers, and cDNA concatamers23. The mim-tRNAseq protocol significantly improved TGIRT-mediated cloning of tRNAs heavily modified internally and at the 3’51, but at the expense of chemical deacylation of 3’ tRNA ends and gel-based size selection25, which could cause unwanted RNA fragmentation26. OTTR-seq26 utilizes the “template jumping” activity of a truncated B. mori R2 reverse transcriptase to synthesize full-length cDNA with 3’ and 5’ adapter sequences. This improved the detection of several RNA classes, including tRNAs27. However, the terminal nucleotidyl transferase activity of the B. mori R2 reverse transcriptase towards the template RNA, necessary for primer duplex hybridization to at least some templates26, requires free (3’OH) ends. Finally, the B. mori R2 RT is not commercially available at the time of writing.

LIDAR captured a synthetic RNA with a biotinylated 3’ with comparable efficiency as a control RNA with a 3’OH, whereas the ligation-based method completely missed the blocked RNA (Fig. 6B). LIDAR libraries from total RNA contained inserts from a variety of small and long RNAs, including full-length tRNAs and tDRs that could not be ligated, likely due to the presence of an amino acid in 3’ (see below). Thus, LIDAR is an effective, ligation-independent alternative to the methods cited above, with the added advantage of its simplicity and the commercial availability of all required reagents.

Revealing elusive 3’ tDRs

Several regulatory functions have been assigned to tDRs43. For example, 5’ tRNA halves generated by angiogenin cleavage sustain cell proliferation in breast cancer cells62. In plants, 3’ tDRs participate in retrotransposon silencing40. tDRs found in mouse sperm have been implicated in the epigenetic inheritance of metabolic disorders41,42. In these reports, ligation-based methods, similar to others commonly used to quantify tDRs63–66, showed a predominance of 5’ tDRs in mature sperm and very few 3’ tDRs. However, 3’ tDRs in sperm were detected by northern blot45, OTTR-seq27, and, now, LIDAR (Fig. 7). Attempts to recapitulate the epigenetic transmission of metabolic information using synthetic 5’ tDRs based on those detected in sperm by ligation-based sequencing was unsuccessful42, indicating that our knowledge of functional RNAs in sperm may be far from complete.

LIDAR detected blocked 3’ tDRs that we speculate to be aminoacylated, although this has only been demonstrated in one example so far59. It is also possible that some of the 3’ tDRs detected by LIDAR may be instead part of nicked mature tRNAs67,68. Finally, some of the “long” (30–40 nts) 3’ tDRs preferentially detected by LIDAR may be 3’ tRNA halves before or after resection or erosion of a few nucleotides at their 5’. Regardless of their origin, we believe that charged 3’ tDRs may represent an overlooked class of RNAs with yet unknown regulatory functions, which LIDAR might help investigate.

Many reads in LIDAR libraries from various sources, including sperm, mapped to mitochondrial tRNAs (Fig. 1F, 7A). Their persistence after enrichment for blocked RNA species (Fig. 7B) suggests that they are charged with amino acids, explaining why ligation-based methods failed to capture them. Interestingly, some shorter reads suggestive of tDRs originating from mitochondrial tRNAs were also detected by LIDAR (Fig. 7B). Non-canonical functions of mitochondrial tDRs have been proposed69,70, but their role is still unclear, especially in the context of sperm maturation and function, and in need of further investigation.

Conclusions and outlook

LIDAR is a powerful, simple, and fast RNA-seq method that allows comprehensive characterization of coding and noncoding transcriptomes from limiting amounts of material with commercially available reagents. LIDAR captured a large variety of RNAs in mouse ESCs, NPCs, brain, testis, and sperm, including full-length tRNAs and 3’ tDRs with blocked 3’ termini that previous methods failed to detect. While the functional implications of the presence of these 3’-blocked tDRs in tissues and sperm are currently unknown, we noticed that they are of the same size as the RNA population responsible for the epigenetic transmission of metabolic phenotypes through the mouse germline41,42.

Full-length tRNAs and tDRs might not be the only RNAs with blocked 3’ ends. Our data suggest that 3’ blocked small RNAs might also be formed from other classes of transcripts, such as snoRNAs and rRNAs (Fig. 6G, S8E). Given that LIDAR has the potential to capture RNAs with any type of 3’ modifications, known or unknown, without the need to remove them, we believe it will become a useful tool to investigate these yet unexplored RNAs.

Limitations of the study

As presented, LIDAR cannot be used on single cells due to adapter dimer contaminations when starting from less than 10 ng of RNA. Post-library depletion methods, such as DASH71,72, might be used to remove adapter dimers and potentially increase sensitivity to the point of allowing LIDAR from single cells. Along similar lines, rRNA depletion steps can be integrated into the LIDAR protocol, increasing sensitivity toward non-rRNA species (Fig. S2A, E). However, care should be taken when performing rRNA depletion, as some methods can introduce biases and cause unwanted RNA fragmentation73. As such, avoiding rRNA removal for LIDAR libraries will preserve the integrity of input RNA and provide a better representation of RNA species, such as 3’ tDRs, present in vivo, at the cost of requiring deeper sequencing. Additionally, it will allow the study of rRNA-derived fragments, which may have biological functions74.

Despite several optimization attempts, some expressed miRNAs could not be detected by LIDAR. One possibility is that the 5’P and 3’OH on miRNAs17 are ideal substrates for ligation, while template switching is more efficient on 5’-capped or 5’OH RNAs75. If detection of the full spectrum of 5’P RNAs were crucial, RNAs could be chemically capped20 or dephosphorylated before LIDAR. Random hexamer priming in LIDAR causes sub-optimal coverage of 3’ ends, which can be improved using 3’-LIDAR (Fig. S3D–H). If full and sensitive coverage only of miRNAs is desired, then a ligation-based method will be more effective than LIDAR.

The ncRNA biotype distribution of LIDAR libraries shows a small bias towards reads mapping to mitochondrial tRNAs compared to cytosolic tRNAs. This might be due to the temperature used for RT (42 °C, as in Smart-seq39), which may favor the lower GC-content of mitochondrial tRNAs76. The reverse transcriptase used in LIDAR is compatible with temperatures up to 60 °C; therefore, the RT conditions could be adjusted.

One type of RNA that might be missed by LIDAR are circular RNAs77, since the lack of a linear 5’ end favors rolling-circle cDNA synthesis over template switching78. To enhance circular RNA detection with LIDAR, it may be possible to enrich circular RNAs and perform gentle hydrolysis79,80.

In addition to 3’ tDRs, LIDAR also revealed the presence of blocked 5’ tDRs (Fig. 7C; S8A). Because, although unlikely, internal priming events on full-length tRNAs generating 5’-tDRs-like reads cannot be formally excluded, we focused our analyses on 3’ tDRs. If a precise and sensitive cloning protocol for 5’ tDRs is desired, a combination of TGIRT-mediated 3’23 and Smart-mediated 5’9 template switches may be the solution. Adding a tagmentation step may also increase coverage of longer RNAs.

METHOD DETAILS

Synthetic RNAs

The synthetic 20 and 50 nts fragments were synthesized by Integrated DNA technologies (IDT) (Table S1). For 3’ biotinylated RNAs, we performed a purification step using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads (Invitrogen, Cat. No 65001) followed by TriPure (Roche, Cat. No 11667165001) purification to ensure all RNAs used in the reaction contained biotin.

NPC differentiation

Prior to NPC differentiation, ESCs were adapted for > 2 weeks to N2B27 serum-free medium consisting of 1:1 mix of neurobasal medium (Gibco, Cat. No 1103049) and DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Cat. No 11320033), supplemented with 1X N2 (Gibco, Cat. No 17502048), 1X B27 (Gibco, Cat. No 17504044), 1% GlutaMAX, 1% non-essential amino acids, 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin, 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 units/mL LIF, and 2i. NPC differentiation was performed as previously described37. Briefly, EpiLCs were induced in N2B27 serum-free medium without 2i and LIF and with 40 μg/mL of BSA and 10 ng/mL bFGF (R&D Systems, Cat. No 3139-FB-025) for 72 h. EpiLCs were then treated with 500 nM SAG (Calbiochem, Cat. No 566661) for 48 h to generate NPCs.

Tissue collection and isolation

Brains and testes were collected from adult (8–12 weeks old) male FVB/NJ mice, rinsed with PBS, and immediately immersed in TriPure. For sperm isolation, cauda epididymides were dissected from adult (8–12 weeks old) male FVB/NJ mice into a dish with 1.5 mL prewarmed Whitten’s media. Caudal fluid was gently squeezed from the tissue and left to incubate in the dish for 10 mins at 37 °C. After incubation, sperm-containing media was transferred to a 1.5 mL tube and sperm was allowed to ‘swim up’ for 10 min at 37 °C. The sperm-containing media was collected, leaving the bottom 50 mL and sperm was pelleted by centrifugation (10,000 g, 5 min, 4 °C). The sperm pellet was washed with 1 x PBS and somatic cells were removed by incubation in somatic cell lysis buffer (0.01% SDS and 0.005% Triton-X) for 10 min on ice. After a final wash in PBS, the sperm pellet was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

RNA extraction and fractionation

For ESCs and NPCs, cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of TriPure and RNA extracted following standard protocol. DNA was digested with Turbo DNase-I (Invitrogen, Cat. No AM2238) at 37°C for 30 min, RNA was re-purified using TriPure, and resuspended in modBTE (10mM Bis/Tris pH 6.7, 0.1 mM EDTA). For mouse tissues, brains and testes in TriPure were homogenized using glass beads and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 1 min. RNA was purified as for ESCs and NPCs. For mouse sperm, total RNA was extracted using the same method previously described for epididymosomes81. Briefly, sperm were resuspended in 120 μL of water and 66 μL of sperm lysis buffer were added [120mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 6.4 M Guanidine-HCl, 5% Tween-20, 5% Triton-X-100, 120 mM EDTA]. Proteins were digested by adding 6.6 μL of 20 mg/mL proteinase K and 6.6 μL of 1M DTT, followed by incubation at 60°C for 15 min under 600 rpm constant shaking. Volume was adjusted with water to 400 μL and 400 μL of TriPure were added. RNA was extracted by adding 120 μL of BCP phase separation reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cat. No BP151) and precipitated with isopropanol. DNA was digested with Turbo DNase-I at 37°C for 30 min, RNA was re-purified using TriPure, and resuspended in modBTE. RNA integrity was verified on 1.5% formaldehyde/agarose gels.

For enrichment of RNAs < 200 nts, the Zymo RNA Clean and Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research, Cat. No R1013) was used, starting from 1 μg of total RNA. For enrichment of RNAs < 50 nts, 18 μg of total RNA were run on a denaturing 12% polyacrylamide-urea gel, and the section between 10 and 50 nts markers was excised. Gel pieces were shredded trough pierced 0.5 mL tubes, and RNA was eluted from the gel by overnight incubation in 400 μL RNA elution buffer (10mM Bis/Tris pH 6.7, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA) at 4°C with constant rotation. Eluate was filtered through 5 μm PVDF spin filters (EMD Millipore, Cat. No UFC30SV00), and RNA was precipitated by adding 1 μL glycoblue (Invitrogen, Cat. No AM9516), 40 μL 3 M NaOAc pH 5.2, and 1.1 mL of ice-cold 100% EtOH, followed by incubation at −80°C for 1 h. RNA was pelleted at 20,000 g for 30 min at 4°C, washed once with 1 mL of 70% EtOH, once with 1 mL of 80% EtOH, air-dried for 5 min, and resuspended in modBTE buffer.

Ribosomal RNA depletion

rRNA depletion with the NEB rRNA depletion Kit (New England Biolabs, Cat No E7400S) was performed starting from 1 μg of total ESC RNA and following the standard protocol, with the exception that rRNA-depleted RNA was purified using TriPure instead of SPRI. rRNA depletion with RiboCop (Lexogen, Cat. No 144.24) was performed starting from 500 ng of total ESC RNA and following the standard protocol, except that the rRNA-depleted RNA were first subjected to Turbo DNase digestion (to remove DNA probes) and purified using TriPure. rRNA depletion with the ZapR module of the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 - Pico Input Mammalian (Takara, Cat No 634485) starting from indexed LIDAR libraries made from 100 ng of total RNA input and following the standard protocol (12 cycles of final PCR).

LIDAR and 3’-LIDAR library preparation

The desired amount of total and fractionated RNAs was diluted to 1 μL and mixed with 0.4 μL of 10 μM LIDAR_RT_primer (see Table S1 for all oligonucleotide sequences). To anneal the LIDAR_RT_primer to the template RNA, samples were heated to 65°C for 5 min, then cooled to 4°C (0.5°C/s). To initiate RT, 6.28 μL of RT_mix [25mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3, 20 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 8 mM DTT, 5% PEG-8000, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 1 mM GTP, 0.5 U/μL RNase inhibitor, murine (New England Biolabs, Cat. No M0314S), and 2U/μL Maxima H-minus RT (Thermo Scientific, Cat. No EP0752); concentrations refer to a final volume of 8 μL] were added and samples were incubated at 25°C for 10 min. To further promote cDNA synthesis and template switch, temperature was raised to 42°C, 0.32 μL of 50 μM LIDAR_TSO_mix (Table S1) were added, and samples were incubated at 42°C for 80 min, followed by 10 cycles at 50°C for 2 min and 42°C for 2 min, then at 85°C for 5 min. To pre-amplify and add adapters to cDNA, 12 μL of KAPA_mix [1X KAPA HiFi HotStart buffer Ready Mix (Roche, Cat. No KK2601), 1 μM LIDAR_preamp_f, and 0.1 μM LIDAR_preamp_r; concentrations refer to a final volume of 20 μL] were added and samples were incubated with the following PCR cycling conditions: denaturation (95°C for 3 min), 18 x (98°C for 20 s, 70°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s), final extension (72°C for 5 min). Quality of adapter-containing libraries was assessed by loading 5 μL on a 2% agarose gel. To generate the final libraries, 2 μL of pre-amplified were diluted to 37 μL with 10mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 0.5 μL of each 10 μM custom Nextera indexing primers was added, followed by 12 μL of Q5_mix [1X Q5 buffer, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 0.02 U/μL Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Cat. No M0491); concentrations refer to a final volume of 50 μL]. Samples were incubated with the following PCR cycling conditions: denaturation (98°C for 30 sec), 18 x (98°C for 10 s, 65°C for 20 s, 72°C for 20 s), final extension (72°C for 2 min). Indexed libraries were purified using 2.3X SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. No B23319) and eluted in 50 μL of TE buffer (10mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA).

For 3’-LIDAR, the LIDAR-3_RT_oligo was generated by mixing equimolar amounts of LIDAR_RT_primer and LIDAR-3_RT_antisense (Table S1) followed by heating at 95°C for 5 min, and a slow cool down to 25°C (0.1°C/s). To prepare 3’-LIDAR libraries, 1 μL of input RNA was first denatured at 70°C for 2 min, then temperature was lowered to 50°C and 0.4 μL of 1 μM LIDAR-3_RT_oligo were added. Samples were incubated at 50°C for further 2 min, then temperature was lowered to 4°C (0.5°C/s). To initiate RT, 6.28 μL of RT_mix were added and samples were incubated at 25°C for 10 min. To further promote cDNA synthesis and template switch, temperature was raised to 42°C, 0.32 μL of 50 μM LIDAR_TSO_mix were added, and samples were incubated at 50°C for 80 min, followed by 10 cycles at 55°C for 2 min and 50°C for 2 min, then at 85°C for 5 min. LIDAR-3_RT_antisense was digested by adding 1 μL of USER II enzyme (New England Biolabs, Cat. No M5508S) and incubating at 37°C for 30 min. USER II was heat inactivated by incubation at 65°C for 10 min. To pre-amplify and add adapters to cDNA, 12 μL of KAPA_mix (1X KAPA HiFi HotStart buffer ready mix, 2 μM LIDAR_preamp_f, and 0.5 μM LIDAR_preamp_r; concentrations refer to a final volume of 20 μL) were added and samples were incubated with the following PCR cycling conditions: denaturation (95°C for 3 min), 6x (98°C for 20 s, 63°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s), 12x (98°C for 20 s, 72°C for 50 s), final extension (72°C for 5 min). Quality of adapter-containing libraries was assessed by loading 5 μL on a 2% agarose gel. To generate the final libraries, 2 μL of pre-amplified libraries were diluted to 37 μL with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 μL of each 10 μM custom Nextera indexing primers was added, followed by 12 μL of Q5_mix (1X Q5 buffer, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 0.02 U/μL Q5 high-fidelity DNA polymerase; concentrations refer to a final volume of 50 μL]. Samples were incubated with the following PCR cycling conditions: denaturation (98°C for 30 sec), 7x (98°C for 10 s, 65°C for 20 s, 72°C for 20 s), final extension (72°C for 5 min). Indexed libraries were purified using 2.4X SPRI beads and eluted in 50 μL of TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA).

LIDAR and 3’-LIDAR libraries were analyzed on a 2% agarose gel and quantified using NEBNext Library Quant Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Cat. No E7630L). Libraries were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq500 or NextSeq1000 instruments.

Northern blots

3 μg (ESCs) or 5 μg (brain, testis) of total RNA were resolved on a denaturing 15% polyacrylamide-urea gel at 180V for approximately 120 min and stained with SYBR gold. RNA was transferred onto positively charged nylon membranes (Roche, Cat. No 11209299001) using a Bio-Rad Trans-Blot turbo system in 0.5X TBE buffer (44.5 mM Tris, 44.5 mM boric acid, 1 mM EDTA; pH 8.3) for 1.5 h at 0.3 A. Membranes were quickly rinsed in 20X SSC buffer (3 M NaCl, 0.3 M Na-citrate, pH 7) and crosslinked at 254 nm to 120 mj/cm2 using a Spectrolinker XL-1500 crosslinker. Membranes were pre-hybridized in ULTRAhyb-Oligo buffer (Invitrogen, Cat. No AM8663) at 60°C for 1 h. Oligo probes (Table S1) were DIG-ylated at their 3’ termini using Terminal Transferase (New England Biolabs, Cat. No M0315S) using the recommended protocol but substituting dNTPs with DIG-11-ddUTP (Jena Bioscience, Cat. No NU-1619-DIGX). DIG-ylated oligo probes were hybridized for 16–18 h at 60°C at a concentration of 10 pmol/mL in ULTRAhyb-Oligo buffer. Membranes were washed twice with 2X SSC (300 mM NaCl, 30 mM Na-citrate, pH 7) + 0.5% SDS for 30 min at 60°C. Membranes were blocked in 1X DIG Northern Starter Kit Blocking Solution (Roche, Cat. No 12039672910) in TBS-T (20 mM Tris pH 7, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated with 1:1000 anti-digoxigenin-AP, Fab fragments (Roche, Cat No 11214667001) in 1X blocking solution in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed twice with TBS-T for 15 min, followed by one wash with CDP-Star buffer (100 mM Tris-Cl pH 9.5, 100 mM NaCl) for 5 min at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with 1 mL of CDP-Star chemiluminescent substrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. C0712–100ML) for 5 min before imaging on an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare).

Enrichment of blocked RNAs

To enrich RNA species with blocked 3’, input RNA (300 ng of total ESC RNA, 75 ng of < 200 nts ESC RNA, or 75 ng of total sperm RNA) was first diluted in 38.5 μL of water and heat-denatured at 70°C for 2 min, followed by quick cool down at 4°C. To generate 5’P and 3’OH ends, 11.5 μL of PNK mix [1X T4 PNK reaction buffer, 1 mM ATP, 1 U/μL T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK; New England Biolabs, Cat no M0201S); concentrations refer to a final volume of 50 μL] were added, and samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. 1 mL of TriPure was added, RNA was purified following the standard protocol, and resuspended in 14.5 μL of water. To polyadenylate the end-repaired RNA, 5.5 μL of EPAP mix [1X E. coli Poly(A) Polymerase Reaction Buffer, 1 mM ATP, 0.25 U/μl E. coli Poly(A) Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Cat. No M0276S), 2 U/μl RNase inhibitor, murine; concentrations refer to a final volume of 20 μL] were added, and samples were incubated at 37°C for 10 min. Polyadenylation reaction was stopped by adding EDTA to a final concentration of 10 mM. To deplete polyadenylated RNAs, 100 μL of Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25 were added to the sample and hybridization was carried at 25°C for 25 min. Flow-through was collected and RNA was purified with TriPure following standard protocol. As control, bead-bound RNA was also extracted using the same procedure. Fractionated RNA was analyzed on 9% denaturing (urea) polyacrylamide gels.

Data processing

mRNA-seq

Adapters were trimmed using TrimGalore with default parameters [ver 0.6.4_dev using Cutadapt version 4.2, https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore and82], retaining reads with a minimum length of 15 bp for both R1 and R2.

Takara SMARTer

Adapters were trimmed using TrimGalore as above. UMIs were extracted from R2 using umi_tools extract (ver 1.1.2)83 with the parameters “--extract-method=string --bc-pattern=X --bc-pattern2=NNNNNNNN.” The remainder of the linker was trimmed using cutadapt with parameter “-u 6.”

PANDORA-seq

Mouse ESC and sperm PANDORA-seq data from28 were downloaded from GEO GSE144666. Adapters are already trimmed from these reads, so no further processing was performed before mapping (below).

NEB

Adapters were trimmed using TrimGalore, retaining paired reads with a minimum length for both R1 and R2 of 5 bp, and R1 singletons with a minimum length of 20 bp (trim_galore –length 5 --paired --retain_unpaired -r 20 -r2 100). The 3’ R2 adapter GATCGTCGG was further trimmed using cutadapt (cutadapt -A GATCGTCGG --minimum-length 5 --pair-filter=any or cutadapt -a GATCGTCGG --minimum-length 5). Paired reads that overlapped by at least 8 bp were connected using COPE (ver 1.2.5)84 in simple connect mode (cope -s 33 -m 0 -l 8). The connection of read pairs at this step results in some paired reads and some single reads, which were processed in parallel during mapping.

LIDAR

Reads were processed with TrimGalore and COPE as above, excluding the 3’ R2 adapter trimming. UMIs were extracted using umi_tools extract (ver 1.1.2)83. To account for the variable length UMIs, three X bases were added to the beginning of each read, then UMIs were extracted with the regex “.*(?<umi_1>.{7})CG(?P<umi_2>.{Consortium, #3344})AG(?P<umi_3>.{Consortium, #3344})GGG”, producing the following patterns for the 4 variable length options, where numbers refer to the six UMI bases:

+0 XXX TG 12 CG 34 AG 56 GGG - insert -> XXXTG123456-insert

+1 XXX VHG 12 CG 34 AG 56 GGG -insert -> XXVHG123456-insert

+2 XXX VATG 12 CG 34 AG 56 GGG - insert -> XVATG123456-insert

+3 XXX VCMTG 12 CG 34 AG 56 GGG - insert -> VCMTG123456-insert

In this way, 151,552 UMIs can be encoded, taking into account the 4 different variable length options, the 0–3 bases preceding the first UMI bases, and the 6 UMI bases.

After UMI extraction, the remaining constant bases (CGAGGGG) were trimmed from the read using cutadapt (cutadapt --minimum-length 5 -g CGAGGGG for single reads or cutadapt --minimum-length 5 -g CGAGGGG --pair-filter=any for paired reads), followed by another trimming step to remove any reads with an occurrence of the TSO or its reverse complement, to stringently remove reads resulting from adapter dimers (cutadapt -e 3 -b CGTCAGATGTGTATAAGA-GACAG --discard-trimmed).

Synthetic RNAs

For samples containing only synthetic RNAs of known sequence, reads were processed as above for LIDAR and NEB, but with a length cutoff of 15 bp required for R1 and R2 during adapter trimming (trim_galore --paired --length 15). Only reads collapsed with COPE were retained, as R1 and R2 for the 20-nt or 50-nt synthetic RNAs should overlap. LIDAR samples were processed to extract UMIs, as described in Read processing section.

The 8N mix (Fig. 1D–E, S1D–E) contained a mixture of 20 and 50 nts synthetic RNA oligos, with the first 12 or 42 bp constant and a random 8 bp following. Reads capturing the 20 nts RNA were retained if they contained the constant first 12 bp with up to one mismatch and had a total length ≤ 20 bp, while reads capturing the 50 nts RNA were retained if they contained the constant first 42 bp with up to three mismatches and had a total length ≤ 50 bp. RPMs were calculated from all reads that passed the adapter trimming step. SeqLogos were generated for the 8N bases by calculating a position weight matrix for all reads with a base at the positions indicated (Fig. S1E).

The 3’OH/3’ blocked oligos mix (Fig 6B, S6A) contained a mixture of 20 and 50 nts synthetic RNAs, some with a 3’OH and others with a blocked 3’ end. After read processing, reads were aligned to a reference composed of the oligo sequences using bowtie2 (ver 2.5.0)85 with default parameters, and LIDAR samples were deduplicated. Only reads with an insert length of ≥ 15 bp were considered. RPMs were calculated from all reads that passed the adapter trimming step.

For the LIDAR vs. 3’-LIDAR comparison (Fig. S3E), all reads with a fragment length ≥ 15 bp mapping to the 3’OH oligos were considered. The plot represents the % of these reads that end at the 3’ end of the oligo.

Mapping (mRNA-seq, NEB, LIDAR, SMARTer, PANDORA)

Reads were mapped in multiple passes to sequentially identify rRNA reads and mapping to ncRNA before mapping to the main genome. Reads mapping at each step were removed before the next step. Only reads with an insert length ≥ 15 bp were retained for downstream analysis.

Locations in the genome predicted to be rRNA repeats were identified using RepeatMasker tracks downloaded from the NCBI Table Browser (group=all talbes, table=rmsk, repClass=rRNA) and masked in the main genome, and a consensus scaffold of rRNA repeats (BK000964.3) was added as a separate scaffold. Genomic loci corresponding to snoRNA and snRNA genes in the GENCODE annotation (ver M27)86, as well as piRNA loci predicted by piRbase (release v3.0 - gold standard set)87 and tRNA from GtRNAdb and tRNAscan-SE, as curated in Behrens et al.25,88,89, were also identified. Locations in the genome corresponding to rRNA and tRNA were masked in the main genome and their sequences were added as separate scaffolds.

For all steps, the paired-end mapping was skipped for reanalysis of PANDORA samples, as these samples were sequenced in a single-end format.

  1. Reads were mapped with STAR (ver 2.7.10a_alpha_220601)90 to a consensus scaffold of rRNA repeats (BK000964.3) with the following parameters: --outFilterMatchNmin 16 -- alignIntronMax 1 --outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.9 --outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.9 --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.05.

  2. Remaining reads were mapped with bowtie2 (ver 2.5.0) to snoRNA sequences described above in --very-sensitive mode. Paired reads mapping in proper pairs with insert size > 15 bp and single reads with insert size > 15 bp were retained as mapping.

  3. Remaining reads were mapped as in step 2 to snRNA.

  4. Remaining reads were mapped as in step 2 to piRNA.

  5. Remaining reads were mapped to tRNA using gsnap (ver 2019-02-26)91, in SNP-tolerant alignment mode with parameters and databases described in in Behrens et al.25,92, including pre-built references of tRNA predictions from GtRNAdb and modifications from MODOMICS93. The following parameters were used: -D <tRNA genome directory> -d <tRNA genome> -V <tRNA genome index> -v <tRNA modification database> --ignore-trim-in-filtering 1 --format sam --genome-unk-mismatch 0 unmapped --md-lowercase-snp --max-mismatches 0.075. Reads mapped concordantly, uniquely or multimapped, were retained and all others were considered unmapped.

  6. Mapping of remaining paired and single reads
    1. Remaining paired reads were mapped to the modified GRCm39 genome, as described above, using STAR with the following parameters: --peOverlapNbasesMin 5 --alignIntronMax 20 --alignIntronMax 100000 --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.05 --outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.9 --outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.9 --outFilterMatchNmin 16. Unmapped reads or reads with insert length smaller than 100 were mapped to the modified GRCm39 genome using bowtie2 in --very-sensitive mode.
    2. Remaining single reads were mapped to the modified GRCm39 genome with bowtie2 in --very-sensitive mode.

  7. All mapped paired reads (rRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, piRNA, tRNA, main genome) were merged, and all mapped single reads were merged.

  8. Samples with UMIs (LIDAR, SMARTer) were deduplicated using umi_tools dedup with --method unique (ver 1.1.2). For these technologies, only reads representing unique molecules were retained.

  9. Only reads with fragment length ≥ 15 bp were included in downstream analyses.

Mapping statistics are reported in Table S2.

Mispriming analysis

The percentage of reads with a mispriming event (Fig. S1H) was defined as the percentage of reads with the UMI sequence (tgNNcgNNagNNGGG) templated in the DNA, indicating a potential TSO strand invasion10. Bases 15 nt upstream of the read start were considered, with 0–2 allowed mismatches.

Read counting for genes and biotypes

A custom annotation was created using RefSeq Annotation Release 109 (GRCm39) with miRNA removed, and miRNA from miRbase (v22)94 added; only mature miRNA were included, and coordinates were converted from GRCm38 to GRCm39 using the LiftOver tool95. Reads mapping to this annotation were counted using an in-house script based on the GenomicRanges (ver 1.50.2)96 function SummarizeOverlaps, counting the number of reads overlapping exons of genes with counting mode IntersectionNotEmpty. The command used to compute counts was assay(summarizeOverlaps(annotation,bam_file, ignore.strand = F, singleEnd = T, param = scanBamParam(flag = scanBamFlag(isSecondaryAlignment = FALSE)), mode = ”IntersectionNotEmpty”)), with singleEnd = F for paired-end reads. Any reads mapping to the ncRNA (snoRNA, snRNA, piRNA, tRNA, rRNA) scaffolds as described above were counted towards the total for those genes. TPMs were calculated to take library size into consideration.

To determine the % of reads in each sample mapping to each biotype (Fig. 1F, 7A, S2D, S2F, S4A, S5A, S6E), all genes for each biotype were collapsed, alleviating issues where multiple genes of one biotype overlapped each other, making read assignment to the specific gene impossible. Any regions of the genome that contained genes of two different biotypes were marked as “ambiguous coding” or “ambiguous non-coding” depending on the presence of a protein-coding gene in that region. Reads mapping to the consensus rRNA scaffold BK000964.3 were ignored for these analyses. The biotypes considered “other” were pseudogene, transcribed pseudogene, misc_RNA, guide RNA, antisense RNA, RNase P RNA, telomerase RNA, RNase MRP RNA, V segment, V segment pseudogene, D segment, J segment, C region, J segment pseudogene, Y RNA, scRNA, and ncRNA pseudogene. Reads mapping to each biotypes were counted as above.

The number of snoRNA, miRNA, and tRNAs detected per million reads (Fig. S2E) was determined by randomly selecting 1 million reads that map to annotated genes for each sample, with proportions reflecting the underlying read count tables, then counting the number of snoRNA or miRNA with at least one read. The number of genes detected in length bins (Fig. S2G) was determined by first generating lists of expressed genes in each length bin, considering any gene with an average TPM ≥ 1 among all samples (LIDAR and ligation libraries; < 50 nt, < 200 nt, and total fractions, n = 3 for each condition). The % of expressed genes in each length bin was then calculated, with a threshold of TPM ≥ 1 for detection.

Differential expression

Differential expression between ESCs and NPCs (Fig. 2) was performed using DESeq2 (ver 1.38.3)97. Genes with an adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Differential expression for tDRs (Fig. 4E, 6H, 7E, S3K–L) was performed only considering reads mapped to the indicated tDRs, generating results that indicate differences in frequency of genes between samples.

tRNA analysis

Classification of tRNA reads into full-length/fragment types

For each tRNA considered in the analysis, the position of each canonical tRNA base, with numbering as provided in Sprinzl et al.98 was annotated using a multiple species alignment of each tRNA to identify canonical bases. Non-templated CCA bases were included in the reference.

Reads mapping to tRNA were classified as full-length, 5’ tRF, 5’ tiRF, internal tRF (i-tRF), 5’ tRF, 3’ tRF, or “other” reads using the following definitions:

  1. Full-length tRNA read: read spans from position 1 at the 5’ end to within 5 bp of the 3’ end (including the CCA) of the tRNA.

  2. 5’ tRF: read spans from position 1 at the 5’ end to ≤ canonical base 31

  3. 5’ tiRF: read spans from position 1 at the 5’ end to ≤ canonical base 35, excluding reads classified as 5’ tRFs

  4. i-tRF: read represents an internal fragment, spanning from ≥ 10 bp from 5’ end and ≥ 10 bp from 3’ end of tRNA

  5. 3’ tRF: read spans from ≥ canonical base 48 to within 5 bp from the 3’ end of the tRNA

  6. 3’ tiRF: read spans from ≥ canonical base 35 to within 5 bp from the 3’ end of the tRNA, excluding reads classified as 3’ tRFs

Misincorporation analysis (Fig. 5A–B, 5E, S6D, S8B)

For each cytosolic tRNA, the percentage of reads with a base differing from the reference or known SNP database was computed for each canonical position, as described above. Only tRNAs with at least 5 reads assigned in all replicates of at least one condition (for example, LIDAR 200 nt ESC RNA) were included in the heatmaps; positions without coverage were considered NA and not included in mean values plotted on the heatmap.

Start and end position of fragments (Fig. 3C, S3J, S5D–E, S7C, S8A)

All reads considered 5’ fragments (reads spanning from position 1 at the 5’ end of tRNA to ≤ canonical base 35) and 3’ fragments (reads spanning from ≥ canonical base 48 to within 5 bp from the 3’ end of tRNA) were considered. The end position in terms of tRNA canonical positions was calculated for 5’ fragments, while the start position was calculated for 3’ fragments.

tRNA coverage by codon (Fig. 6D, S5C)

For each tRNA, the coverage for each canonical position, as described above, was calculated. Coverage over the variable region was collapsed by taking the mean of all variable positions. In the case of a tRNA missing canonical bases, coverage was calculated as the mean of the closest present canonical bases to the left and right. Coverage was then collapsed by anticodon by taking the sum of all tRNAs with each anticodon and converted to RPMs based on the total number of reads mapped in each sample.
