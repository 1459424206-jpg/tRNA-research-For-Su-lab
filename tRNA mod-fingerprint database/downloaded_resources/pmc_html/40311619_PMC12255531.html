Summary

Chemically modified nucleotides in mRNA are critical regulators of gene expression, primarily through interactions with reader proteins that bind to these modifications. Here, we present a mechanism by which the epitranscriptomic mark N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is read by tRNAs during translation. Codons that are modified with m6A are decoded inefficiently by the ribosome, rendering them “non optimal” and inducing ribosome collisions on cellular transcripts. This couples mRNA translation to decay. 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl-2-thiouridine (mcm5s2U) in the tRNA anticodon loop counteracts this effect. This unanticipated link between the mRNA and tRNA epitranscriptomes enables the coordinated decay of mRNA regulons, including those encoding oncogenic signaling pathways. In cancer, dysregulation of the m6A and mcm5s2U biogenesis pathways - marked by a shift towards more mcm5s2U - is associated with more aggressive tumors and poor prognosis. Overall, this pan-epitranscriptomic interaction represents a novel mechanism of post-transcriptional gene regulation with implications for human health.

Introduction

The epitranscriptomic mark m6A decorates transcripts from thousands of metazoan genes and influences their processing and metabolism1,2. This includes pre-mRNA splicing, mRNA export, localization and translation. By far the most prominent function of m6A is, however, its impact on mRNA decay3.

m6A is co-transcriptionally installed by the m6A-methyltransferase core complex (MTC) consisting of METTL3, METTL14 and WTAP and the associated factors RBM15, VIRMA, CBLL1 and ZC3H13 4. The MTC interacts with RNA polymerase II, modified histone H3K36 and transcriptional cofactors. In the nascent RNA, the catalytic subunit of the complex, METTL3, methylates the m6A consensus sequence motif DRACH (D denotes A, G or T; R denotes A or G and H denotes A, C or T). Interestingly, only a small fraction of the DRACH motifs in the transcriptome are modified5, because the exon-junction complex (EJC) shields DRACH motifs from methylation, thereby directing m6A to long internal exons and the terminal exon6–8. This leads to an uneven distribution of m6A along mRNAs, with an enrichment around the stop codon (often referred to as the m6A “metagene”). However, how this nonrandom pattern affects the function of m6A is currently unknown.

The effects of m6A are thought to be mediated by ‘reader’ proteins that bind to the mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner. Although the molecular mechanisms triggering and regulating m6A-mediated mRNA decay remain incompletely understood, it has been shown that YTH domain family proteins (YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3) mediate this process3,9. They recruit the CCR4/Not deadenylase complex, and it is thought that this initiates the decay of m6A-containing mRNAs. Furthermore, it has been proposed that mRNAs are transferred between YTHDF1 and YTHDF2, with the former promoting mRNA translation and the latter promoting mRNA decay10. However, how these activities are coordinated to prevent the premature decay of an mRNA before it is translated is currently unknown3,9.

Here, we show a positional effect of m6A where m6A in the protein coding sequence (CDS) coordinates mRNA translation with mRNA decay and is tuned by tRNA modifications. By combining reporter assays and transcriptome-wide analyses, we show that this depends on the translation of m6A-containing codons by elongating ribosomes and occurs in a codon-specific manner. Ribosome profiling reveals that a subset of codons is decoded inefficiently when m6A-modified, suggesting that m6A reduces codon optimality and thereby marks mRNAs for increased decay. The tRNA anticodon modification mcm5s2U modulates this hidden layer of the genetic code by alleviating the decrease in codon optimality caused by m6A. Depletion of mcm5s2U results in even stronger translational pausing at m6A-modified codons and more rapid decay of m6A-modified mRNAs. Importantly, we observe ribosome collisions in response to m6A deposition, suggesting a potential mechanism for m6A-mediated decay that is independent of specific reader proteins. Functionally, mRNAs whose turnover is tuned by m6A and mcm5s2U are enriched for oncogenic signaling pathways. Tumors show a higher expression of mcm5s2U biogenesis factors relative to m6A biogenesis factors and this is associated with increased tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis.

Results

m6A in the CDS increases mRNA decay

The presence of m6A sites increases the decay rate of many cellular mRNAs, but it is currently unknown whether all m6A sites of an mRNA contribute equally to this effect. To test whether positional effects influence m6A-mediated decay, we used miCLIP5 to comprehensively map m6A in HEK293T cells. The single nucleotide resolution of this map allowed us to unambiguously assign each m6A site to the different regions of the mRNA (Figure 1A). We then stratified mRNAs according to the localization of their m6A sites and analyzed their decay rates11. This revealed that mRNAs with m6A exclusively in their 3’UTR do not decay faster than those without m6A but are even slightly more stable (Figures 1B & S1A). In contrast, mRNAs with m6A in their CDS had significantly higher decay rates (Figures 1B & S1A). To confirm this result in a different organism and cell type, we determined transcriptome-wide mRNA decay rates using actinomycin D and RNA sequencing in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) and matched them to a published m6A map12. Similar to human cells, mRNAs containing m6A in the 3’UTR were more stable than those with m6A in the CDS (Figures 1B & S1A). This suggests that in mouse and human cells, m6A positioned in the CDS is the main driver of m6A-mediated mRNA decay.

m6A is not evenly distributed throughout the mRNA body. Rather, it is deposited away from exon-exon junctions6–8, resulting in an enrichment of m6A around the stop codon1,2. It has been hypothesized that this characteristic distribution of m6A is linked to its function. Therefore, we next tested whether it is associated with different mRNA decay rates. We used a high-resolution map of m6A that contains information on the methylation stoichiometry of individual m6A sites13. This allowed us to quantify the amount of m6A across the length-normalized mRNA to obtain a quantitative m6A metagene. We then stratified mRNAs according to their decay rate and plotted the average distribution of m6A for the different decay rates. This revealed a striking enrichment of m6A in the CDS of highly unstable mRNAs, whereas the CDS of stable mRNAs was largely devoid of m6A (Figure 1C). Accordingly, the characteristic enrichment of m6A around the stop codon was most pronounced in stable mRNAs, which have low total m6A levels but are highly expressed (Figure S1B). Therefore, the stop codon peak in the m6A metagene is primarily derived from stable mRNAs whose CDS is depleted of m6A. In contrast, unstable mRNAs showed a much higher proportion of m6A in the CDS (Figure 1C), which also correlated better with increased decay than m6A in the 3’UTR (Figure S1C).

To experimentally validate the causal relationship between the presence of m6A in the CDS and mRNA decay, we generated a series of reporter constructs. To control the positioning of m6A, we designed DRACH-less reporter mRNAs and inserted a cassette of twelve methylatable DRACH motifs downstream of an mNeonGreen open reading frame. This cassette was located either at the end of the CDS or at the beginning of the 3’UTR, depending on the codon immediately preceding the DRACH cassette (Figure 1D). Introduction of a CGA sense codon placed the cassette in the CDS, whereas a UGA stop codon placed it in the 3’UTR. These reporter mRNAs differ only by a single nucleotide, but the length of their CDS is different. Since this can also influence mRNA decay, we generated an additional construct with a mutated DRACH cassette (Figure 1D). This reporter mRNA cannot be m6A-modified but has a CDS of identical length. We then transfected these constructs into HEK293T cells, blocked transcription with either triptolide or actinomycin D, and monitored reporter mRNA decay by RT-qPCR. Consistent with our genome-wide results, the reporter with m6A in the CDS was the least stable (Figure 1E).

To verify that this was indeed caused by m6A, we treated reporter-expressing cells with STM2457 14, a small molecule inhibitor of METTL3. Whereas the reporter without m6A was unaffected, this led to a destabilization of the reporter with m6A in the 3’UTR (Figure 1F). Such a stabilizing effect of m6A has been described before15 and is consistent with our observation that cellular mRNAs with m6A exclusively in their 3’UTR are more stable than those without m6A (Figures 1B and S1A). Most importantly, however, STM2457-treatment resulted in a marked stabilization of the CDS m6A reporter, which was now almost as stable as the reporter without m6A (5.9 h vs. 6.0 h; Figure 1F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that m6A in the CDS triggers rapid mRNA turnover.

m6A couples mRNA translation to mRNA decay

We next asked which process might underlie this positional effect of m6A. Since positioning of m6A in the CDS accelerates mRNA decay, we hypothesized that ribosomal translation mediates this process. To test this, we treated reporter-expressing HEK293T cells with the translation inhibitors cycloheximide or emetine. Both led to a pronounced stabilization of the CDS m6A reporter (Figure 2A and S2A), confirming that its decay is translation dependent. The other reporters were also stabilized, but this effect was more variable and (with the exception of the cycloheximide-treated 3’UTR m6A reporter) weaker. Thus, while we cannot exclude a general effect of translation elongation on mRNA decay16, the decay of reporter mRNAs with m6A in their CDS is clearly driven by their translation.

To test whether translation elongation is similarly required for the decay of endogenous m6A targets, we analyzed Klf4 and Nanog in mESCs. These pluripotency factor mRNAs are well-established targets of m6A-mediated decay17 and contain multiple m6A sites in their CDS (Figure S2B). In emetine-treated wild-type (WT) cells, their decay was strongly reduced and comparable to that in Mettl14 KO cells (Figure S2B). Thus, translation is required for the m6A-dependent clearance of pluripotency factor mRNAs in mESCs, a physiological function of m6A17. To confirm this on a transcriptome-wide scale, we measured mRNA decay in emetine-treated mESCs using RNA sequencing. mRNAs with m6A in their CDS were the most stabilized, to the extent that they were as stable mRNAs without m6A (Figure 2B). To confirm this in human cells and enable a more quantitative analysis, we analyzed mRNA decay data from emetine-treated HEK293T cells18 together with data on m6A stoichiometry in these cells13. This revealed a highly significant correlation between the m6A level of an mRNA and its stabilization by emetine (Figure S2C). Notably, while the effect was small at the level of the individual mRNA, it was observed across the transcriptome, as 4120 out of 10564 genes were stabilized by more than 20% (Figure S2C). Accordingly, a striking correlation between the average m6A level and the translation-mediated decay of an mRNA was observed (Figure 2C). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the m6A-dependent decay of large portions of the transcriptome is mediated by the translation of an m6A-containing CDS.

m6A impairs decoding in vivo

The dependence of m6A-mediated decay on translation suggested a role for the ribosome in this process. Several translation-dependent mRNA decay pathways are associated with inefficient decoding and slow translation elongation. For instance, extreme pauses induce No-Go Decay (NGD), where endonucleolytic cleavage is triggered at stalled ribosomes19. A more subtle but genome-wide effect is the reduction of mRNA half-life that is induced by non-optimal codons20,21. Since m6A can interfere with tRNA selection in the aminoacylation site (A site) of bacterial ribosomes in vitro22, we hypothesized that inefficient decoding may underlie m6A-mediated decay.

To test this idea, we analyzed whether m6A-modified codons are inefficiently decoded in vivo by using our miCLIP m6A map to identify codons that are m6A-modified in HEK293T cells and performed ribosome profiling in these cells. In ribosome profiling, increased occupancy of a codon at ribosomal A-sites indicates that it is being decoded more slowly23. To exclude potential bias from differential codon usage between different transcripts24, we used our m6A map to generate stringent control sets of matched codons: For each m6A-modified codon, we selected the nearest unmodified codon of the same type in the same CDS. To control for effects caused by the DRACH motif itself rather than by m6A (e.g., due to inefficient codon pairs25,26), we performed this control step twice, selecting unmodified codons that either were within a DRACH motif or not. Thus, for each codon we generated three sets: (i) m6A-modified codons, (ii) unmodified codons in a DRACH motif, and (iii) unmodified codons not in a DRACH motif. A comparison of these three sets showed that m6A-modified codons have indeed higher ribosome occupancies than unmodified codons in vivo (Figure 3A), an effect we also observed using two published ribosome profiling datasets27,28 (Figure S3A). Furthermore, this effect was not caused by the presence of m6A-binding proteins, as we observed a reading frame-specific A-site offset (Figure S3B). This reading frame analysis also confirmed that m6A is positioned at the A-site of slow ribosomes and not elsewhere in their mRNA tunnel (Figure S3B). Thus, even within the same CDS, m6A-modified codons are decoded more slowly than unmodified instances of the same codon.

To experimentally confirm that this effect is due to m6A, we performed ribosome profiling in HEK293T cells treated with STM2457 to inhibit m6A formation. Strikingly, this reduced the occupancy of codons that are normally m6A-modified to baseline levels (Figure 3A). It also suggested that miCLIP was generating false negative DRACH motifs, as this codon group was sensitive to STM2457 (Figure 3A). To confirm these results with an m6A map generated by a different methodology and gain a more quantitative understanding of the effect, we calculated m6A stoichiometries for individual codons13. In untreated cells, there was a strong and significant correlation between m6A stoichiometry and codon occupancy (R2=0.66), which was not observed in STM2457-treated cells (R2<0.01; Figure 3B).

Therefore, modification of codons with m6A deoptimizes their decoding by mammalian ribosomes in vivo.

m6A induces codon-specific effects

Due to the DRACH recognition motif, METTL3 can modify nine sense codons with m6A (Figure 3C). We therefore examined whether the decoding of these methylatable codons is affected by m6A to the same extent or whether there are codon-specific effects. Importantly, the occupancy of eight out of nine methylatable codons was increased when the codon was m6A-modified (Figure 3D), and this effect was present at thousands of codons across the transcriptome (Figure S3C). Thus, m6A does indeed lead to a general impairment of decoding in vivo. However, the magnitude of the effect was strikingly different between codons: For example, the GGA codon had a low occupancy when unmethylated and its decoding was unaffected by m6A (Figure 3D). In contrast, GAA codons already had a high occupancy in the unmodified state, and this was further increased when the codon was modified (Figure 3D). Intriguingly, these codons differ in the strength of their codon-anticodon interaction: GGA is an inherently strong codon, with two G-C base pairs forming three hydrogen bonds at the first and second codon positions29. In contrast, GAA is a codon of intermediate codon-anticodon interaction strength, as only one G-C base pair is formed29. In general, intermediate codons with only one G-C base pair at the first two codon positions appeared to be more “responsive” to m6A: While their occupancy was relatively low when unmethylated, it increased substantially when the codon was modified with m6A (Figure 3D and 3E). Importantly, this effect was almost completely reversed in STM2457-treated cells (Figure 3E). Therefore, codons are differentially affected by m6A, and the magnitude of the effect is determined by the strength of the codon-anticodon interaction.

mcm5s2U in the tRNA anticodon decodes m6A

Codon-anticodon interactions are modulated by tRNA modifications in the anticodon loop29,30. We noticed that m6A at the third position of a codon induces stronger m6A-dependent pauses than m6A at the first two positions (Figure 4A). The third codon position is special, because it allows non-Watson-Crick (or “wobble”) base pairing with the anticodon31. Interestingly, codons with m6A at the wobble position are decoded by tRNAs containing the modified nucleotide 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl-2-thiouridine (mcm5s2U) at uridine 34 (U34), the nucleotide that base pairs with the wobble base in mRNA (Figure 4B). Since mcm5s2U leads to more efficient decoding32–34, we speculated that it might facilitate decoding of m6A-modified codons.

To test this idea, we generated HEK293T cell lines in which one of the mcm5s2U biogenesis factors, ELP1 or CTU2, was knocked out using CRISPR-Cas9. In the case of ELP1, the initial carboxymethylation step of the hierarchical mcm5s2U biogenesis pathway is prevented, leaving unmodified U (Figure S4A). In the case of CTU2, its inactivation blocks the final thiolation step, leaving mcm5U and ncm5U. We confirmed that the tRNAs in both KO cell lines are devoid of mcm5s2U (Figure S4B) and analyzed codon occupancies in these cells using ribosome profiling.

To verify that our analysis captured codon-specific changes in ribosome occupancy, we first analyzed the codon-specific effects expected from inhibition of ELP1 or CTU2. Global decoding efficiencies were unaltered in the KO cell lines, as mcm5s2U-independent codons did not show increased occupancies (Figure S4C). As expected, mcm5U-dependent codons showed increased occupancies in ELP1 KO, but not CTU2 KO cells (Figure S4D), whereas mcm5s2U-dependent codons showed increased occupancies in both cell lines (Figure S4E). Thus, our ribosome profiling faithfully recapitulated codon-specific changes in decoding efficiency.

We then assessed the impact of mcm5s2U-deficiency on m6A-modified codons, focusing on the subset of methylatable codons that are also mcm5s2U-dependent: AAA, AGA, and GAA (Figure 4B). For all three, the correlation between m6A stoichiometry and ribosome occupancy was strikingly more pronounced in mcm5s2U-deficient cells (Figure 4C). This was most evident for the AGA codon, where m6A has little effect in WT cells but results in strong ribosome pauses in mcm5s2U-deficient cells (Figure 4C). Therefore, it appears that the AGA codon is the most sensitive to a lack of mcm5s2U. Importantly, this increase in A-site occupancy was reversed in STM2457-treated cells (Figure 4C), demonstrating that m6A and mcm5s2U functionally interact at the A-site of translating ribosomes in vivo, where mcm5s2U remedies the de-optimizing effect of m6A.

It has been proposed that inefficient decoding leads to the formation of collided ribosomes, which may act as a binding platform for the CCR4/Not complex that initiates mRNA decay35,36. To test whether ribosome collisions occur at m6A-modified codons (Figure 4D), we profiled colliding ribosomes in WT, ELP1 KO and CTU2 KO cells. While we did not observe collisions at AAA codons, they were present at AGA and GAA codons in WT cells (Figure 4E and S4F). For the inherently inefficient GAA codon, they were also present (albeit at low levels) at unmodified codons (Figure S4F), whereas for the m6A-responsive AGA codon, they were only detected at m6A-modified codons (Figure 4E). These collisions were caused by m6A, as they were reduced by STM2457 treatment (Figure 4E and S4F). Importantly, collisions at AGA and GAA were increased in mcm5s2U-deficient cells (Figure 4E and S4F). Furthermore, this mcm5s2U-dependent increase was not observed at unmodified AGA codons or when cells were treated with STM2457 (Figure 4E), confirming the complex interplay between m6A and mcm5s2U that influences decoding. Notably, depletion of mcm5s2U did not activate the ribotoxic stress response37 (Figure S4G), suggesting that the localized collisions at m6A-modified codons are physiological inducers of mRNA decay.

If the functional interaction of m6A and mcm5s2U regulates mRNA decay, the depletion of mcm5s2U should accelerate the decay of mRNAs containing m6A-modified codons. To test this hypothesis, we transfected ELP1 and CTU2 KO cell lines with a reporter construct in which the DRACH cassette in the CDS uses an AGA codon. Since AGA requires the full mcm5s2U modification, the decay of this reporter mRNA is expected to respond to the depletion of ELP1 and CTU2. Indeed, the half-life of the reporter was reduced in both KO cell lines compared to wild type (Figure 5A). This decay was driven by m6A, as STM2457 treatment stabilized the reporter mRNA (Figure 5A). Of note, even in the presence of STM2457, reporter decay was slightly faster in KO than in WT cells. This likely indicates that the reduced decoding efficiency of unmodified AGA codons also contributes to the accelerated reporter decay in mcm5s2U-deficient cells.

To further dissect the codon-specific mechanisms that trigger m6A-mediated decay, we generated a reporter in which the AGA codon in the DRACH cassette was replaced by GGA. While AGA and GGA can both be m6A-modified, decoding of the inherently ‘strong’ GGA codon is not affected by m6A, as evidenced by the lack of effect of m6A on A-site codon occupancy (Figure 3D and S4H). Consistently, we did not observe ribosome collisions at m6A-modified GGA codons (Figure S4F). In line with this, the AGA reporter was less stable than the GGA reporter (Figure S4I), and this effect was due to m6A, as treatment with STM2457 reduced the difference in ribosome occupancy and reporter half-life between the two codons (Figure S4H and S4I). Thus, the codon-specific effect of m6A on mRNA decay correlates with the decoding efficiency of the codon.

Finally, we tested whether the decoding of GGA can also be modulated by the modification status of the tRNA. While AGA requires mcm5s2U, the GGA codon is decoded by a tRNA containing mcm5U that is not 2-thiolated. Thus, it requires the activity of ELP1 but not CTU2 for its physiological modification profile. Accordingly, the decay of the GGA reporter was accelerated in ELP1 KO cells, but not in CTU2 KO cells (Figure 5B). Therefore, combinatorial effects between m6A and mcm5s2U at specific codons control mRNA decay.

mcm5s2U and m6A control the expression of oncogenic pathways

To test whether the interplay between m6A and mcm5s2U also affects the decay of endogenous mRNAs, we measured the effect of m6A inhibition in mcm5s2U-deficient cells. Treatment of ELP1 KO and CTU2 KO cells with STM2457 for 4h resulted in an upregulation of mRNAs that was dependent on the level of m6A in the CDS but not the 3’UTR (Figure 5C). The effect of STM2457 in WT cells was much weaker, suggesting that m6A-mediated decay is enhanced in mcm5s2U-deficient cells (Figure 5C). Measuring decay rates of cellular mRNAs using SLAM-Seq38 indeed revealed an accelerated decay of m6A-modified mRNAs in ELP1 and CTU2 KO cells (Figures S5A and S5B). While m6A levels correlated well with the rate of destabilization in CTU2 KO cells (Figure S5A), a more global destabilization affecting also mRNAs with little m6A was observed in ELP1 KO cells (Figure S5B). This effect could be due to pleiotropic effects on additional codons that require mcm5U but not mcm5s2U for efficient decoding (such as GGA, see above). Nevertheless, these results show that the depletion of mcm5s2U impacts the decay of a substantial fraction of the transcriptome because it is m6A modified.

Since this form of mRNA decay integrates the activities of multiple epitranscriptomic marks, we coined the term “pan-epitranscriptomic”. To identify a physiological role for this pan-epitranscriptomic mRNA decay mechanism, we focused on the functions of the affected mRNAs. An analysis of cancer hallmark gene sets39 revealed a striking difference in m6A-dependent decay between two major functional classes: Metabolic pathways with housekeeping functions are characterized by stable mRNAs that contain little m6A (Figure 5D). In contrast, mRNAs encoding components of signaling pathways contain high levels of m6A and are rapidly degraded (Figure 5D). These highly modified, unstable signaling-pathway mRNAs were preferentially stabilized in emetine-treated cells (Figure S5C). Thus, m6A controls the expression of key signaling pathways by coupling translation of their mRNAs to rapid decay. Strikingly, these pathways were destabilized even further in mcm5s2U-deficient cells (Figure S5D and S5E). Therefore, mcm5s2U counteracts the effects of m6A and stabilizes the mRNAs of important signaling pathways, providing a “licensing” mechanism for a more sustained expression of these tightly controlled pathways.

The mcm5s2U/m6A balance is a cancer biomarker

Consistent with such a reciprocal licensing function, studies have shown that m6A promotes the decay of mRNAs encoding pluripotency factors, thereby facilitating stem cell differentiation17,40, while mcm5s2U supports the expression of pluripotency factors, enabling stem cell maintenance41,42. Since sustained expression of pluripotency genes and oncogenic signaling pathways promotes tumor stemness43, we tested whether the mcm5s2U/m6A system might play a role in human cancer. In normal human tissues44, the expression of mcm5s2U biogenesis factors relative to m6A biogenesis factors correlated with tissue stemness43 (Figure S5F). Intriguingly, a shift towards mcm5s2U was also observed in tumor samples from the TCGA pan-cancer panel45 (Figure S5G). Thus, increased stemness and tumorigenesis are associated with a shift in the mcm5s2U/m6A licensing system (Figure 5E).

Since increased tumor stemness is associated with poor prognosis43, we assessed whether the mcm5s2U/m6A licensing system can serve as a biomarker. We focused on breast cancer because increased levels of mcm5s2U have been described in breast cancer cells and increased decoding efficiency of GAA codons was linked to breast cancer metastasis46,47. We therefore calculated univariate hazard ratios for individual mcm5s2U and m6A biogenesis factors in the METABRIC breast cancer patient cohort48. In striking agreement with their opposing molecular functions, mcm5s2U factors were associated with increased risk whereas m6A factors were associated with decreased risk (Figure 5F). This was confirmed in the independent SCAN-B breast cancer patient cohort49 (Figure S5H), suggesting that a shift in the mcm5s2U/m6A licensing system is associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients.

Finally, we calculated a pan-epitranscriptomic gene expression signature using the coefficients of the METABRIC model. This signature predicted patient survival in both breast cancer cohorts (Figure 5G and S5I) and stratified breast cancer molecular subtypes50 (Figure S5J and S5K). A shift towards mcm5s2U was consistently associated with poor patient survival and more aggressive tumor subtypes. In addition, the signature stratified patients in different TCGA cohorts, including lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and blood cancer (Figure S6). Also in those patients, a shift towards mcm5s2U was predominantly associated with poor prognosis (Figure S6). This indicates a role for the mcm5s2U/m6A licensing system in multiple human cancers and suggests that it may serve as a pan-cancer biomarker.

Discussion

Chemically modified nucleotides tune the activities of different RNA species such as mRNA, tRNA and rRNA. Here, we show that two such nucleotide modifications in different RNA species interact at the molecular level in cells to exert an in vivo function.

Using different m6A maps at single-nucleotide resolution, we found that mRNA decay correlates with m6A levels in the CDS (Figure 1 and S1) and occurs in a translation-dependent manner (Figure 2 and S2). By combining ribosome profiling with the use of a small molecule inhibitor of METTL3, we show that translation of most codons becomes less efficient when modified by m6A (Figure 3 and S3). This effect of m6A is partially alleviated by modification of the corresponding tRNA by mcm5s2U (Figure 4 and S4). Thus, the reciprocal interaction of tRNA and mRNA modifications emerges as a key determinant of mRNA half-life to maintain physiological gene expression extending previous findings51. This mechanism controls the decay of thousands of mRNAs including transcripts encoding important oncogenic signaling pathways (Figure 5 and S5).

Such post-transcriptional RNA regulons have been previously described and proposed to be controlled by the coordinated actions of RNA-binding proteins or micro RNAs52. Here, we describe a mechanism based on modified nucleotides in mRNA and tRNA (Figure 6A). In principle, this mechanism could be completely independent of trans-acting factors other than the translation- and decay machineries. Therefore, it will be interesting to investigate whether m6A readers such as the YTHDF proteins act via a different pathway or whether they mediate or modulate this form of m6A-mediated decay caused by slow ribosomes. Regardless, translation-dependent decay as described here offers an elegant solution to explain why m6A-modified mRNAs are not prematurely degraded prior to translation.

The finding that m6A “imprints” mRNAs with decay information that is read by the ribosome also provides a mechanistic explanation for how numerous low stoichiometry m6A sites might be functional: Since the per-codon elongation rate of non-optimal codons is integrated at the transcript level to mediate mRNA decay53, even small changes that occur at many lowly methylated codons can trigger ribosome collisions and thereby affect an mRNA’s decay rate. Thus, total m6A levels across the entire coding sequence, rather than at specific positions, are likely to determine mRNA decay rates. While this is an attractive model, m6A- and mcm5s2U-modified nucleotides do not need to physically interact directly to be effective. Both modifications may act together by altering the local environment of stacking and base paring interactions29. Mechanistically, slow ribosomes with unoccupied A sites at non-optimal codons are recognized by the Ccr4/Not complex, which initiates mRNA decay35. Whether this mechanism also degrades mRNAs with ribosomes at m6A-modified codons is currently unknown. However, the detection of colliding ribosomes at m6A-dependent stalling sites (Figure 4D and S4F) suggests that this process contributes to decay. Interestingly, the m6A readers YTHDF1, YTHDF2 and YTHDF3 directly bind Ccr4-Not3. This raises the attractive possibility that YTHDF proteins stimulate translation-dependent mRNA turnover by enhancing the recruitment of Ccr4-Not to slow ribosomes. Alternatively, m6A reader proteins may directly affect ribosomal decoding, as has been proposed for the YTHDC2 helicase54.

The tRNA anticodon loop is a hotspot for chemical RNA modifications, and therefore other combinations of m6A-modified codons and modified tRNA anticodons exist. One such example is the decoding of AAC and GAC by tRNAs containing queuosine (Q) in their anticodon (Figure 6B). The micronutrient Q can only be synthesized by prokaryotes and is inserted into eukaryotic tRNAs by QTRT1 and QTRT2. Codons ending with AC (“NAC” codons) are translated more efficiently in cells with inactivated QTRT1 or QTRT2 55, suggesting that Q is a “deoptimizing” tRNA modification. Intriguingly, we find that this effect also depends on m6A (Figure 6C). Notably, for the AAC codon, which was found to be insensitive to Q 55, increased A-site occupancy is seen exclusively at m6A-modified codons (Figure 6C). These findings underscore the complex nature of pan-epitranscriptomic interactions and prompt further research into their combinatorial effects on transcriptome stability. The synthesis of tRNA modifications requires multiple metabolic pathways and they have therefore been proposed to act as sensor or transducers to link the metabolic state of a cell to translation56. By integrating different branches of their metabolism into epitranslatomic interaction networks, cells likely gain the ability to subtly control gene expression, also beyond the key modification pathways identified in our study. It will be important to understand the full extent of the interactions between tRNA and mRNA modifications.

tRNAUCUArg carries mcm5U in unicellular eukaryotes and lower metazoans such as worms and flies57. In vertebrates, however, tRNAUCUArg carries mcm5s2U 58, and it has been unclear what led to the evolutionary shift in its modification pattern. The observation that the AGA codon shows the strongest effect on m6A-dependent mRNA translation and decay likely provides an evolutionary explanation for why vertebrates have expanded their tRNA modification spectrum. The striking deoptimization of AGA by m6A suggests that 2-thiolation of tRNAUCUArg has been an evolutionary “innovation” of vertebrates to utilize m6A in their CDS while minimizing negative effects on translation efficiency. Such a mechanism would have allowed vertebrates to utilize the mcm5s2U/m6A system to mediate mRNA turnover without the negative effects on protein homeostasis that would be expected downstream of codon-specific translation defects32. The analysis of additional pan-epitranscriptomic interactions in different organisms will help to better define the evolutionary relationship between different RNA modifications.

It is becoming increasingly clear that mcm5s2U plays a role in tumorigenesis, metastasis and therapy resistance in many cancers46,59. Our findings suggest that this may be due to a common mechanism, namely the stabilization of mRNAs from multiple oncogenic signaling pathways that would otherwise be curtailed by m6A. Therefore, codon biases observed in cancer cells may not function by directly modulating protein synthesis, but rather by tuning mRNA turnover60. Finally, chemical modifications are used in therapeutic mRNAs to modulate innate immunity and mRNA stability61. The finding that such modifications functionally interact with tRNA modifications opens new possibilities for tuning mRNA function and stability in therapeutic mRNAs and for exploiting tumor vulnerabilities that may arise from their specific tRNA modification status.

Limitations of the study

Tuning m6A-mediated mRNA decay by mcm5s2U affects a substantial fraction of the transcriptome, but effect sizes at the level of individual mRNAs are difficult to quantify. First, while ribosome occupancy correlates with decoding efficiency, cellular quality control systems that degrade mRNAs with paused or collided ribosomes affects the reads recoverable by ribosome profiling. Second, it is unclear how effects at individual m6A sites are integrated at the gene level, e.g., whether two codon positions methylated in 50% of the mRNAs of a gene have the same effect on its average decay rate as one codon position methylated in 100% of the mRNAs. Third, while our reporter genes and experimental perturbations demonstrate that m6A is a major factor responsible for the observed changes in mRNA decay, it will be key to establish a quantitative framework to assess these effects at the level of individual genes. This is particularly important given the time-dependent effects of mRNA decay: In the absence of transcriptional buffering, any change in the decay rate of an mRNA will have twice the effect on its expression level if measured over twice the time.

We used different single-nucleotide m6A maps, both published and experimentally determined by us. However, they were generated using wild-type cells in steady state. Therefore, we cannot exclude that changes are induced at individual m6A sites when cells are treated with drugs or depleted of mcm5s2U. Since this may affect individual m6A sites with varying efficiencies, our measurements may over- or underestimate the true effect at individual codons.

The cancer association of the mcm5s2U/m6A system is primarily based on a correlation between RNA modification gene expression and multiple indicators of tumor aggressiveness. This is valid because the expression levels of RNA modifying enzymes typically correlate with the corresponding modification level of the target RNA62. However, further studies, including direct quantification of modified nucleotides in patient RNA, are needed to fully establish the role of the mcm5s2U/m6A system in carciogenesis.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mammalian cell culture

HEK293T cells and derived ELP1 KO and CTU1 KO cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated superior Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Merck) and 1x Penicillin-Streptomycin-Glutamine (Thermo Fisher) at 37 °C in 5 % CO2. Cells were passaged every 3–4 days by seeding 3.×106 cells into a 10 cm dish. Media were changed every other day. Wild-type and Mettl14 KO mESCs were grown in knockout DMEM (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 15% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 10U/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher), 1x GlutaMax (Thermo Fisher), 500 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher), 1x Nonessential amino acid (Thermo Fisher), 103 U/mL LIF (Sigma-Aldrich), 3 μM CHIR99021 (Thermo Fisher), and 1 μM PD0325901 (Thermo Fisher). Cells were seeded on a plate coated with EmbryoMax™ 0.1% Gelatin Solution (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were passaged every day using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Generation of mcm5s2U-deficient cells

A quadruple nicking strategy was used to reduce off-target effects67,76. Briefly, Cas9-D10A nickase and guide RNAs (gRNAs) were encoded on the pX335A-G2P plasmid. During gRNA design, the offset between each gRNA pair was set below 20 nt and the deletion size was set below 500 bp. CHOPCHOP was used to select high-confidence gRNA pairs68. The pX335A-G2P-gRNA constructs were prepared using standard cloning procedures. The pX335A-G2P-gRNA constructs were transfected into HEK293T cells using FuGENE HD (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One day before transfection 0.2×106 cells/well were seeded into one well of 6-well plates. Transfection was performed at a transfection reagent:DNA ratio of 2.5:1. 16 h after transfection, cells were washed with 1x PBS and fed with fresh media containing puromycin (5 μg/ml). Cells were selected for 24 h and allowed to recover for 3 d. After recovery, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1-cell per well. Once the cell clones were confluent, half of the cells were used for genomic DNA isolation and screening, while the other half of the cells were seeded into one well of a 6-well plate. Deletions were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The absence of mcm5s2U in both cell lines was verified by northern blot and RNA mass spectrometry.

METHOD DETAILS

miCLIP-based m6A mapping

miCLIP libraries were prepared as described previously5. Raw sequencing reads from all miCLIP datasets (see the Key Resource Table) were concatenated, aligned with STAR to GRCh38/hg19 and de-duplicated using UMI-tools. Alignment was performed in end-to-end mode to assess the intra-read distribution of C to T transitions. Transitions were quantified using Rsamtools pileup and m6A-sites were called by requiring at least 2 transitions and a transition rate between 4 % and 40 %.

Binning of mRNAs by m6A position

mRNA region bins.

For human and mouse transcripts, m6A sites from our miCLIP census (or from ref.12) were counted for each gene and transcript region (5’UTR, CDS and 3’UTR). Genes with m6A sites exclusively in one region were annotated for that region, as well as those with m6A in both the CDS and the 3’UTR. Genes with m6A in the CDS and the 5’UTR were therefore excluded from further analysis.

Quantitative m6A metagene.

mRNAs were binned according to their half-lives (from ref.11) and relative mRNA coordinates. Then, the sum of the GLORI m6A stoichiometry (from ref.13) was calculated for each bin.

Quantitative m6A bins in CDS and 3’UTR.

For the quantitative binning used in Figure 5C, the sum of the GLORI signal (from ref.13) in the CDS and 3’UTR was summed up for each gene.

Plasmid-based reporters for m6A positional effects

All reporters were designed so that the expressed mRNAs (including promoter regions and termination signals) do not contain DRACH motifs or mcm5s2U-dependent codons unless intentionally introduced. All elements were synthesized (GenScript) and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) (Thermo Fisher) by standard cloning or Gibson assembly. An mCherry gene in which all m6A DRACH motifs and mcm5s2U-dependent codons were replaced by synonymous codons was used as an expression control. N-terminally V5-tagged mNeonGreen free of DRACH motifs and mcm5s2U-dependent codons followed by a cassette of 12x DRACH or 12x DRAGH motifs was cloned under the control of a CMV promoter. For each codon-specific cassette, the three DRACH-containing sequence motifs containing the codon of interest with the highest m6A level based on GLORI (ref.13) were cloned in sequence and repeated 4 times. To generate the 3’UTR reporter, a CGA codon between mNeonGreen and the 12x DRACH motif was replaced by a TGA stop codon.

mcm5s2U detection by northern blot

1 μg total RNA was heated at 80 °C for 5 min and applied to denaturing polyacrylamide gels (10 % polyacrylamide (19:1), 7 M urea, with or without 0.05 % ([N-acryloylamino]phenyl)mercuric chloride (APM) in 0.5 × TBE; ref.64). RNA was transferred to Immobilon NY+ membranes (Millipore) by semi-dry transfer with 0.5 × TBE. Northern blot was performed essentially as described using the following DNA oligo probes77:

tRNAUUULys: CCTGAACAGGGACTTGAACCCTGA

tRNAUUGGln: GGTCCCACCGAGATTTGAACTCGG

tRNAUUCGlu: GTTCCCACACCGGGAGTCGAAC

tRNAUCUArg: CACTCACGATGGGGGTCGAACCCA.

Signals were acquired and analyzed using a Fuji FLA 7000 phosphoimager (Fujifilm). Digital image analysis was performed using Fiji-ImageJ2 (The Fiji Project).

Western blot

Treated and control cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150 mM KCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 1 % Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cell Signaling), EDTA-free Protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)) and resolved on a 7% SDS-PAGE gel supplemented with 10.7 μM Phos-tag (Wako Chemicals) and 21.3 μM MnCl2. The separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore) using wet electroblotting (180 V and 400 mA) for 1.5 h. Membranes were blocked in 5 % dry-milk and the primary antibodies (anti-Zakα, 1:4000; anti-p38, 1: 1000; anti-α-tubulin, 1:1000) were applied at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the membrane was washed three times in TBST and incubated with the secondary antibodies (HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit, 1:20000; HRP-conjugated anti-mouse, 1:10 000) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed three times in TBST and incubated with ECL Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva) and signals detected using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems).

Ribosome profiling

Generation of ribosome profiling libraries.

Ribosome profiling was conducted essentially as described78,79. For steady-state measurements, wild-type, ELP1-KO, and CTU1-KO HEK293T cells were grown in triplicate in 10 cm dishes and supplied with fresh medium 2 h before lysis. For treatment with STM2457 (DC Chemicals), 10 cm plates were seeded with 5×106 wild-type or 6×106 ELP1-KO and CTU1-KO HEK293T cells in triplicate. After 16 h cells were treated with either 10 μM STM2457 or 1 % DMSO for 4 h. In both cases, cells were harvested by rinsing once with ice-cold PBS and snap frozen by placing the dish in liquid nitrogen. The dish was then placed on wet ice and 400 μl lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 % Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5 % deoxycholate (w/v) and 100 μg/ml CHX) was added dropwise to the cells. The cells were scraped from the dish as soon as the lysis buffer was thawed. Next, the samples were centrifuged at 10’000 g at 4 °C for 5 min. 10 A260 units of the supernatant were digested with 800 U or 900 U RNaseI (Thermo Fisher) for 1 h at 22 °C and shaken on a thermoblock at 1’400 rpm. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 15 μl SuperaseIn (Thermo Fisher). Digested lysates were separated on a sucrose gradient, and fractions containing monosomes and disomes were collected and loaded on a gel. 28–32 nt (monosome) or 56–64 nt (disome) footprints were excised from the gel and used to generate ribosome profiling libraries78. Therefore, RNA was dephosphorylated using T4 PNK (NEB). The DNA linker was ligated to the 3’ end of the RNA with T4 RNA Ligase 2, truncated KQ. The linker was randomized at the first 4 positions of their 5’ end to reduce potential ligation biases80. Reverse transcription was performed with SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher). The cDNA was circularized using CircLigase II (Biosearch Technologies). Library PCR was performed using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher).

Analysis of ribosome profiling libraries.

Monosome and disome footprints were processed by removing the adapter sequence and randomized barcode at the 5’- and 3’ ends using cutadapt (v3.7). Clean reads were filtered by mapping to non-coding RNAs (e.g. snRNA, rRNA, snoRNA, tRNA, etc.). The remaining reads were mapped to the CDS of canonical transcripts (Ensembl genes 111) using bowtie (v1.3.1). CDSs were extended by 21 nt to allow mapping of initiating and terminating ribosomes. For ribosome profiling, 29–31 nt (monosomes) or 58–63 nt (disomes) long footprints were selected for downstream analysis. The A-site offset for each read length was assigned according to the 5’ end of the initiating ribosome footprints. For disome profiling, the A-site offset for each read length was assigned according to the 5’ end of the terminating disome footprints (the stalling ribosome is the terminating ribosome). The normalized read coverage for each codon was calculated by normalizing the raw counts of that codon by the read density of the CDS (the first 15 and the last 15 codons are excluded from the analysis to avoid effects that might stem from initiation or termination):

A-sitecoverage=cijΣL-1515cij/(L-30)

Where Cij is the read count of codon i in gene j;L is the length of CDS (codons).

To calculate the A-site coverage for codons in different groups (“no DRACH”, “DRACH”, “m6A”) or groups based on GLORI stoichiometry13, the mean coverage of all m6A or mcm5s2U codons in each group/bin was calculated using genes with read density >0.1.

Analysis of published ribosome profiling data.

Prior to alignment with STAR69, potential adapter sequences were trimmed from downloaded fastq files with flexbar74 and footprints with a length of 28, 29 or 30 nt after trimming were used. Offsets from RPF 5’-ends to A-site codons were determined empirically by analyzing RPF 5’-end coverage and were 15 nt for all datasets. Normalized A-site coverages were then determined by calculating a z-score in a window of +/− 60 nt centered on each nucleotide in the longest annotated CDS for each gene. Codon occupancies were defined as the normalized A-site coverage at codon position 1 (i.e., that of ribosomes translating the codon in-frame).

SLAM-Seq

SLAM Seq was performed as described previously38. Briefly, wild-type, ELP1-KO and CTU1-KO HEK 293T cells were grown in 100 μM 4-thiouridine (Tocris) for 1h or 2h and harvested by adding 300 μl Trizol (Invitrogen) directly to the dishes. RNA was isolated, treated with iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich), and sequencing libraries were generated using QuantSeq 3′ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Lexogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SLAM-Seq reads were processed using GRAND-SLAM81. Reads with at least two T to C transitions were considered as derived from “new” RNA and reads without transitions were considered derived from “old” RNA. These “new” and “old” reads were summed for each gene set or exon length bin and the “new to old” ratio was used to calculate decay rates as described previously81.

mRNA decay measurements in mESCs

mRNA half-life was measured as previously described with some modifications82. Cells were grown to ~80% confluency. Cells were grown in the absence or the presence of 10 ug/mL actinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h or 3 h. As control, cells were treated with DMSO for 3 h. -Total RNA was harvested using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher. Purified RNA was quantified using a Qubit4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher). 1 μg of RNA was mixed with ERCC RNA Spike-In Mix (Thermo Fisher). Ribosomal RNA was first depleted using the NEBNext® rRNA Depletion Kit (NEB). RNA-seq was then prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB). Library quality was assessed on Agilent TapeStation4200 using High Sensitivity DNA ScreenTape (Agilent). Libraries were quantified on a Qubit4 fluorometer using the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) prior to sequencing.

RNA sequencing of STM2457-treated cells

Raw Quant-seq reads were processed by removing the poly-A at the 3’ end using cutadapt (v3.7) after extracting the 12 nt UMI. Clean reads were mapped to the human genome (hg38) using STAR (v2.7.10b). PCR duplicate reads were removed using UMI-tools (v1.1.2). Read counts for each gene were quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.3) with default parameters. Transcript per million (TPM) was calculated for each gene.

For reporter mRNA decay measurements, 0.14×106 cells/well of each genotype were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected with 250 ng/well of reporter plasmid using FuGENE HD (Promega) according to manufacturer’s instructions. 24 h post-transfection, cells were treated with the following inhibitors for time-course experiments: 10 μM STM2457 (DC Chemicals), 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 μM emetine (Sigma-Aldrich), 80 μg/mL cycloheximide (Sigma-Aldrich). m6A and mcm5s2U gene expression signatures

Tissue gene expression data were downloaded from GTEx44. Normalized gene expression matrices for matched tissue and tumor samples from the TCGA project were obtained from the recount2 project83. Samples from the METABRIC48 and SCANB49 breast cancer cohorts were obtained from cBioPortal84 and the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), respectively. For the mcm5s2U/m6A balance score, the relative expression of mcm5s2U thiolation factors (CTU1, CTU2, MOCS3, URM1) to MTC core factors (METTL3, METTL14, WTAP) was calculated.

To assess RNA modification activities in the samples, the median expression of gene sets for in m6A biogenesis (METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, RBM15, ZC3H13, CBLL1, VIRMA) and mcm5s2U biogenesis (ELP1 to ELP6, ALKBH8, CTU1, CTU2, MOCS3, URM1) were calculated. The activities of the individual mcm5s2U carboxymethylation (ELP1 to ELP6, ALKBH8) and thiolation (CTU1, CTU2, MOCS3, URM1) steps were similarly assessed. The mRNA expression-based stem cell signature (mRNAsi) was calculated as described previously43.

Breast cancer survival analysis

The METABRIC cohort48 was used as a training cohort and gene expression and clinical data were obtained from cBioPortal84. The SCAN-B cohort was used as a validation cohort49, and gene expression and clinical data were obtained from GEO. Cox proportional hazards were calculated for overall survival (OS) using the R package “survival”85. The combined m6A/mcm5s2U signature was calculated using the coefficients obtained in the univariable Cox regression of the METABRIC training cohort.
