SUMMARY:

Protein and RNA acetylation are crucial for development and cancer progression. NAT10 is the only known acetyltransferase responsible for N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification of RNA. However, the mechanism by which NAT10 contributes to cancer progression remains unclear. Here, we show that NAT10 interacts with a mechanosensitive, metastasis-susceptibility protein complex at the nuclear pore. Loss of NAT10’s acetylation activity significantly reduces lung metastasis in both allograft and genetically engineered mouse models of breast cancer. Unexpectedly, upon NAT10 knockout, loss of ac4C-modification in chromatin-associated tRNAs disrupts p300/CBP function, resulting in genome-wide chromatin reorganization and altered expression of genes that recruit metastasis-promoting myeloid cells to the tumor microenvironment. These findings highlight a role for NAT10 in regulating enhancer activity in metastatic tumor cells and reveal its impact on tumor-immune interactions that contribute to metastatic progression.

Keywords: Metastasis, Acetylation, tRNA, Chromatin, Enhancer, Transcription, Tumor Microenvironment, Neutrophils

INTRODUCTION:

Metastasis, a complex process by which cancer cells spread to distant organs, remains one of the most formidable challenges in oncology1,2. Metastasis is associated with most cancer-related deaths and limits the success of existing therapeutics 3. Numerous genetic1,4, epigenetic5, and microenvironmental factors6 have been implicated in the metastatic cascade, underscoring the need for a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Previously, we demonstrated that genetic polymorphisms play an important role in metastatic potential7. Most inherited polymorphisms are found in noncoding regions of DNA and are thought to alter phenotypes by modifying gene expression rather than directly affecting protein function. Genome-wide analysis of metastasis-associated polymorphisms have demonstrated that polymorphisms can predict metastasis outcomes in both human estrogen receptor (ER) positive8 and ER negative9 breast cancer patients, leading to the identification of multiple metastasis susceptibility genes, including Sipa110, Rrp1b11, Brd412, Arntl29 and Nup2108. Interestingly, many of these factors colocalize at the nuclear lamina or nuclear pore8,13 where the nuclear pore protein, NUP210, facilitates mechanosensation from the extracellular microenvironment through a SUN2-associated LINC complex14. Previous mass spectrometry analysis also suggested that NAT10 may interact with this complex at the nuclear lamina13. However, a potential role for NAT10 in cellular mechanosensation and metastasis has not been previously defined. In this study, the role of NAT10 in metastasis progression was therefore investigated.

NAT10 has previously been implicated as a cytidine acetyl transferase (ac4C) for rRNAs, tRNAs15, mRNAs 16 and as a protein acetylase17–19. The role of NAT10 in cancer has garnered increasing interest, with mounting evidence implicating it in both cancer development20–23 and metastatic progression24–27. However, most studies have focused on acetylation of individual substrates. In this study, we employed an unbiased approach that combines genomics, proteomics, and transcriptomics to explore the role of NAT10 in metastasis. Our findings reveal a previously unknown mechanism of NAT10 modulating p300/CBP activity via chromatin-associated tRNA, resulting in enhanced metastatic potential of tumor cells through crosstalk with innate immune myeloid cells to form a metastasis-promoting tumor microenvironment.

RESULT:

Depletion of NAT10 significantly decreases lung metastasis in mice.

To assess a possible role of NAT10 breast cancer progression, AURORA US Metastasis Project28 data was analyzed and revealed elevated expression of NAT10 in metastases compared to the primary tumor (Figure 1A–B). Survival analysis revealed an association of NAT10 expression with poor distant metastasis-free survival outcome in ER+, but not ER− patients (Figure 1C–D). However, RNA-seq-based datasets revealed that high expression of NAT10 is associated with poor overall survival of basal, luminal-B and Her2+ PAM50 subtypes (Figure S1A) consistent with a contributory role. To directly test the role of NAT10 in metastasis, mouse allograft-based spontaneous metastasis assays were performed. Orthotopic transplantation of Nat10 shRNA knockdown (KD) mouse mammary tumor cells significantly decreased primary tumor weight (Figure 1E–F) and lung metastases in 4T1 cells (Figure 1G). In addition, Nat10 KD in another mouse cell line (6DT1) (Figure S1B) showed a similar trend toward decreased lung metastasis, without altering primary tumor weight (Figure S1C–D). To validate this using an orthogonal strategy, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout (KO) of Nat10 in 4T1 cells was performed (Figure 1H), although complete knockout was not achieved, as previously observed16. Orthotopic implantation of two independent Nat10 KO clones (c19 and c43) showed inconsistent changes in primary tumor weight (Figure 1I), however, both clones significantly decreased lung metastases (Figure 1J). Conversely, overexpression of Nat10 in 4T1 significantly increased primary tumor weight (Figure S1E–F) and caused a trend toward increased lung metastasis (Figure S1G). Similar findings were observed in 6DT1 cells, though primary tumor weight was not altered (Figure S1H–I). Finally, C57BL/6 mice carrying an inactivating H537A point mutation in the Nat10 acetyltransferase domain (Meier et al., manuscript in preparation) were crossed with the highly metastatic PyMT mammary tumor model (Figure 1K). Dot blot analysis of spleen RNA from H537A mice confirmed that ac4C level is reduced in H537A heterozygous mutant compared to WT animals (Figure S1J). Consistently, mice carrying one copy of the mutant Nat10 allele had significantly decreased lung metastases (Figure 1L, 1M) and metastatic incidence (Figure 1N), confirming a role of NAT10’s acetyltransferase activity in metastatic progression.

Translational efficiency is not impaired in NAT10-depleted tumor cells.

Recently, NAT10 has been implicated in increased protein translation efficiency through mRNA ac4C-acetylation16. To assess this as a possible mechanism for the metastatic phenotype, the Nat10 KO cells were examined. As expected, dot blot analysis revealed significantly reduced ac4C levels in Nat10 KO 4T1 cells (Figure 2A). A sequencing-based method demonstrated specific loss of ac4C in 18S rRNA (Figure 2B), and a ~30% reduction of ac4C-modified tRNA was also observed by immuno-northern blot (Figure 2C). Western blot revealed a slight increase in both total p70S6 kinase and phospho-p70S6 kinase levels in Nat10 KO cells, indicative of intact translational control pathways (Figure 2D). Furthermore, Nat10 KO did not induce translational stress in this cell line as indicated by unaltered level of phospho-EIF2α. Consistent with this, O-propargyl-puromycin (OPP) assays on NAT10-depleted cells showed an increase in nascent protein synthesis (Figure 2E). These results suggest that the mechanism by which NAT10 facilitates metastasis in our system is unlikely to be via deacetylated mRNA-dependent suppression of protein translation.

NAT10 depletion phenocopies disruption of the mechanosensitive NUP210-dependent metastasis pathway

Unexpectedly, NAT10-depleted cells (Figure 2F, S2A–C) exhibited an adhesion defect on both glass and plastic matrices , similar to that observed for NUP2108. Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) and proximity ligation assay (PLA) confirmed association of NAT10 with the previously described NUP210-bound nuclear pore complex (Figure 2G–I, S2D–F), suggesting a common mechanistic role. Interestingly, depletion of NAT10 was associated with a significant decrease of NUP210-associated mechanosensitive genes (Figure 2J–K), suggesting that NAT10 may function upstream of the NUP210 mechanotransduction pathway. Like NUP210-depleted cells, immunofluorescence revealed redistribution of histone H3.1/3.2 to H3K9me3-enriched heterochromatin foci after NAT10 depletion (Figure 2L). Moreover, like NUP210 and NAT10, other members of this putative complex were responsive to extracellular matrix stiffness (Figure 2M). Taken together, these data are consistent with NAT10 functioning together with NUP210 at the nuclear pore as part of metastasis-associated mechanosensitive pathway.

NAT10 depletion leads to decreased histone acetylation.

In addition to its known RNA ac4C acetylase function, NAT10 has been reported to auto-acetylate29 as well as acetylate UBF17, tubulin30, p5318, and MORC219 proteins. Therefore, unbiased mass spectrometry-based analysis was performed to detect additional potential NAT10 protein targets. Nuclear immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry revealed 361 proteins (2-fold enrichment over IgG) potentially interacting with NAT10 in 4T1 cells (Figure 3A–C; Table ST1A–D). To identify potential direct acetylation targets, proteome-normalized acetylated lysine peptide enrichment was performed in WT and Nat10 KO 4T1 cells (Table ST2A–E). Acetylation of histones was mostly downregulated in Nat10 KO 4T1 cells (Figure 3D). Overlap analysis of the two datasets revealed that nine NAT10-interacting proteins had reduced acetylation in NAT10-depleted cells (Figure 3E), including multiple histone proteins.

Pan-acetylated lysine (K) immunoprecipitation followed by western blot revealed decreased acetylation of three major histone variants (H3.1/3.2, H3.3 and H4) in Nat10 KO 4T1 cells (Figure 3F). NUP210 or BRD4 isoforms were not pulled down in Pan-ac-K Co-IP (Figure S2G–H) suggesting that they are not NAT10 acetylation target. Western blot further revealed decreased H3 and H4 acetylation without a significant alteration of heterochromatin modifications (H3K9me3 and H3K27me3) in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 3G). To test whether NAT10 can directly acetylate histone H3 and H4, in vitro histone acetyl transferase activity (HAT) assays were performed using recombinant human NAT10. p300, but not NAT10, was able to acetylate histones under these conditions (Figure 3H–I, Figure S2I). Treatment of 4T1 cells with either a p300/CBP inhibitor (A-485) or p300/CBP PROTAC degrader (dCBP-1) revealed that p300 is the main HAT for H3K27ac/H4K20ac modifications (Figure 3J). Addition of NAT10 to HAT assays did not alter the autoacetylation levels of the p300/CBP (Figure S2J–K) or another histone acetyltransferase, KAT7 (Figure S2L) suggesting that NAT10 does affect the activation of these HATs. Furthermore, addition of NAT10 did not affect the ability of p300 to acetylate histone H3 (Figure S2M). Together, this data therefore suggests an indirect role of NAT10 in regulation of histone acetylation.

NAT10 depletion promotes p300/CBP upregulation and altered localization.

Since NAT10 depletion resulted in decreased acetylation of p300/CBP targets, we assessed p300/CBP protein levels in KO cells. Unexpectedly, p300, CBP levels and acetylated p300/CBP were significantly upregulated in Nat10 KO 4T1 (Figure 4A) and 6DT1 shRNA knockdown cells (Figure S3A). Ectopic expression of NAT10 in KO cells partially decreased p300/CBP upregulation (Figure 4B). RNA-seq analysis revealed that p300 mRNA, but not Cbp was significantly upregulated in Nat10 KO cells (Figure S3B), potentially implicating both transcriptional and translational/stability effects. To eliminate the artifacts due to stable NAT10 depletion, acute depletion was performed in HEK293FT cells using dTAG degron tagging system 31, which also resulted in upregulation of p300 (Figure 4C). Rescue in the KO cells with an acetylase-inactivating H537A point mutation demonstrated that the H537A mutant could not restore ac4C to the same extent as WT NAT10 (Figure S3C). However, both wild-type and H537A mutant NAT10 significantly decreased p300 levels in Nat10 KO cells (Figure S3D), suggesting either moderate rescue of ac4C activity or re-expression of the protein itself was sufficient to rescue p300 levels. To address this question, NAT10 deletion mutants were generated to identify potential protein domains necessary for increased p300 level (Figure S3E). However, this question remained unresolved due to the instability or low expression of the deletion constructs. Inducible expression of NUP210 in Nat10 KO cells did not alter p300 level implying that NAT10 is not mediating its effect on p300 via NUP210-dependent pathways (Figure S3F).

To determine whether p300 localization is altered in Nat10 KO cells, immunofluorescence analysis was performed. p300 was found to be partially localized in DAPI-stained heterochromatin regions in KO cells (Figure 4D). Quantification of p300 signals in Nat10 KO and rescued cells revealed that p300 intensity significantly increased and was colocalized with H3K9me3 in Nat10 KO cells, which was reversed by re-expression of NAT10 (Figure 4E–F). PLA further confirmed the significant increase of the interaction of p300 with H3K9me3 (Figure 4G). To test whether the inactivation of p300 is linked to its increased level and altered localization, 4T1 cells were treated with the p300 inhibitor A-485 or the p300/CBP degrader dCBP-1. Western blot showed increased p300 but reduced H3K27ac in A-485–treated cells (Figure S3G). Immunofluorescence further revealed enhanced nuclear p300 intensity and relocalization to H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin (Figure S3H). Consistent with p300 inactivation, the negative regulatory p300 phospho-S8932 was elevated in Nat10 KO cells, but not completely decreased by NAT10 re-expression (Figure S3I), suggesting it is not a direct effect of NAT10 loss. Phosphatase treatment did not reduce p300 levels (Figure S3J), suggesting that the increase in p300 is not solely due to phosphorylation but rather reflects a downstream consequence of p300 upregulation.

Recent work shows that p300 HAT activity can be reduced by occlusion of active sites in condensates that colocalize with H3K27me333. In Nat10 KO cells, H3K27me3 intensity was increased and colocalized with p300 (Figure S3K), consistent with the hypothesis that reduced p300 HAT activity may be due to the occlusion of active site. p300 also showed enhanced colocalization with the nuclear speckle marker SC35 (Figure S3L), indicating accumulation in inter-chromatin space. These results suggest that NAT10 is required for proper p300 acetylation and localization.

NAT10 interacts with p300 at enhancers.

To assess whether NAT10 mediates its effect on histone acetylation via interaction with p300, co-IP with endogenous or Flag-tagged NAT10 in 4T1 cells was performed. Co-IP revealed that both Flag-tagged and endogenous NAT10 physically interact with p300 (Figure 4H). Immunofluorescence revealed that nucleoplasmic and perinucleolar NAT10 colocalized with p300 and H3K27ac enhancer marks, but not with H3K9me3 (Figure 4I). PLA confirmed the NAT10-p300 association and revealed that the interaction is comparable to p300 interaction with H3K27ac (Figure 4J). Furthermore, PLA revealed that NAT10 was found to interact with H3K27ac and H4K20ac enhancer, but not with H3K9me3 heterochromatin marks (Figure 4K). 3D reconstruction revealed that many interactions were nucleoplasmic, near heterochromatin foci (dark blue) and the nucleolar periphery (dark zone surrounded by heterochromatin foci) (Figure 4L). To further validate these interactions, ChIP-seq was performed. NAT10 was found diffusely enriched in the H3K27ac/H4K20ac enhancer regions and depleted at transcription start sites (TSS) (Figure 4M). Interestingly, ChIP-seq of other components of nuclear pore-bound mechanosensitive protein complex revealed that these proteins were enriched in the Myc super-enhancer region as well as Nup210 and Ets1 enhancer regions (Figure S4A). These results suggest that NAT10 interacts with p300 along with other nuclear pore-bound mechanosensitive proteins at enhancer regions.

NAT10 depletion disrupts enhancer organization.

Since p300 is essential for histone acetylation dependent enhancer activation34, enhancer organization was examined in Nat10 KO cells. ChIP-seq analysis of p300, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac revealed that some promoters exhibited reduced occupancy while others displayed increased enrichment, suggesting a redistribution of these marks in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 5A). Notably, H3K27ac-marked enhancer regions (excluding TSS ±2 kb) showed a significant reduction in p300, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac enrichment (Figure 5B). Global read distribution analysis further revealed an overall decrease in H3K27ac and H4K20ac levels (Figure 5C), consistent with the western blot and mass spectrometry analyses. In contrast, p300 read distribution showed minimal overall decrease, suggesting that chromatin-bound p300 levels are only moderately reduced. Interestingly, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 heterochromatin marks showed decreased enrichment in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 5D). Given that reduced heterochromatin marks may correlate with increased chromatin accessibility, we performed ATAC-seq analysis in Nat10 KO cells. While global chromatin accessibility around the TSS had approximately equal numbers of sites with increased or decreased accessibility, ~10-fold increase of accessibility was observed in enhancer regions (Figure 5E–F). Consistent with these findings, PLA showed significantly increased interaction between p300 and H3K27ac in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 5G).

Differential binding analysis within TSS ±2 kb revealed both upregulated and downregulated p300 and H3K27ac peaks (Figure 5H), further supporting the redistribution of these marks. Peak distribution analysis identified 1,999 gene promoters with ≥1.5-fold reduction and 929 with ≥1.5-fold increase in H3K27ac enrichment in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 5I, Table ST3A–D). Promoters of cell adhesion and migration genes showed reduced H3K27ac, whereas developmental and differentiation genes displayed increased H3K27ac. At the locus level, chromatin accessibility remained largely unchanged at gene-proximal enhancers that showed reduced enrichment of p300, H3K27ac and H4K20ac (Figures 5J, S4B). In contrast, Tnfrsf19 and several putative non-coding RNA genes (Gm29771, Gm36831, Gm40173, Gm29836) with enrichment of active chromatin marks exhibited increased accessibility and reduced levels of H3K9me3 and H3K27me3. Additionally, in Nat10 KO cells, elevated H3K9me3 levels were detected in some constitutive heterochromatin domains (Figure S4C), which may explain the increased H3K9me3 signal observed by immunofluorescence. Differential enrichment of H4K20ac around TSS mirrored the pattern seen with H3K27ac (Figure S4D–E, Table ST4A–D).

To verify whether NAT10 depletion alters overall enhancer organization, we performed 3D DNA FISH analysis to visualize the intra-nuclear localization of the Myc super-enhancer regions, which are amplified in aneuploid 4T1 cells (Figure 5K). Quantification revealed that Myc superenhancer loci became closer to the heterochromatin foci in Nat10 KO cells and was accompanied by decreased Myc mRNA and protein expression (Figure 5L–M). Treatment of 4T1 cells with a p300 inhibitor A-485 treatment also downregulated Myc mRNA (Figure S4F) and protein expression (Figure S4G). Overall, these results are therefore consistent with the depletion of NAT10 resulting in alteration of p300 localization and disruption of enhancer organization.

NAT10 depletion increases p300 binding to chromatin associated RNAs (caRNA)

Recent evidence suggests that RNA binding to the p300 paralog CBP stimulates histone acetylation at the enhancers 35. In addition, caRNAs (including lncRNAs, nascent RNAs and enhancer RNAs) have been shown to facilitate functional genomic interactions to regulate gene expression 36. To determine whether Nat10 KO affected p300-caRNA interactions, we isolated chromatin and performed p300 RNA-ChIP (Figure 6A). Globally, p300 binding to caRNAs was markedly increased in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 6B–D). Most of the p300-interacting caRNAs were nascent transcripts, originating from either enhancer regions (eRNAs) or pre-mRNAs. Interestingly, despite the global increase in p300-caRNA interactions, p300 enrichment was reduced at some enhancer RNAs. Unexpectedly, genes that lose (Itgb1, Nup210, Fbln2) or gain (Tnfrsf19, Scara5) H3K27ac enhancer marks exhibited increased p300 binding to their pre-mRNAs. caRNA-seq analysis revealed that these pre-mRNAs were far less abundant than chromatin-associated long non-coding RNAs (e.g., Malat1) and were further reduced in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 6E). This suggests that the p300-bound nascent pre-mRNAs in Nat10 KO cells might be rapidly degraded by the nuclear RNA exosome making them underrepresented in the total caRNA population. Nascent RNAs have been shown to interfere with the binding of chromatin regulators to nucleosomes37. Therefore, in Nat10 KO cells, p300 might be sequestered by excessive binding to these nascent RNAs, potentially explaining the paradoxical decrease in histone acetylation despite elevated p300/CBP protein levels in KO cells.

NAT10-mediated acetylation of chromatin-associated tRNAs is associated with altered p300/CBP activity

Based on the data above, we hypothesized NAT10 may acetylate caRNAs that could alter p300/CBP localization and activity. To investigate this, caRNAs from WT and Nat10 KO cells were analyzed using dot blot and immuno-northern blot. Dot blot analysis revealed reduced ac4C signal in the caRNA fraction of Nat10 KO cells (Figure 6F). Unexpectedly, a significant enrichment of small RNAs, such as tRNAs and 5S rRNAs, in the DNase I–digested caRNA fraction was observed (Figure S5A). Immuno-northern blot showed a marked reduction in ac4C levels at chromatin-associated tRNAs (Figure 6G), suggesting that ac4C-modified tRNAs are deacetylated, destabilized, or evicted from chromatin upon NAT10 loss. To directly assess whether loss of tRNA acetylation affects p300 protein levels, a time-course experiment using HEK293FT NAT10-dTAG degron-tagged cells was conducted. A modest but noticeable increase in ac4C signal at the tRNA band at 6 hours post-NAT10 depletion was observed (Figure 6H). However, ac4C levels began to decline at 24 hours and were nearly undetectable by 48 and 72 hours, coinciding with impaired cell proliferation and adhesion (Figure 6I). Western blot revealed a transient increase in p300 levels at 6 hours, followed by a plateau at 24 hours and a pronounced upregulation at 48 and 72 hours (Figure 6J, S5B). Similarly, H3K27ac and H4K20ac levels increased slightly at 6 hours, then gradually declined at later time point. These findings suggest that p300 levels and activity are dynamically regulated in response to the loss of NAT10-mediated tRNA acetylation.

NAT10 acetylates serine (Ser) and leucine (Leu) tRNAs, which has been linked to their stability38. To investigate whether disruption of tRNA levels can influence p300/CBP protein levels, we treated 4T1 cells with ML-60218, an RNA polymerase III inhibitor that broadly suppresses Pol III–mediated transcription of small RNAs, including U6 snRNAs, 5S rRNAs, tRNAs, and the long non-coding RNA Rn7sk. qRT-PCR revealed that treatment for 48 hours did not significantly reduce the steady-state levels of these RNAs, but significant reduction was observed after 96 hours (Figure S5C). In contrast, tRNA ac4C signal showed a slight but significant increase at 48 hours (Figure S5D) followed by a decrease at the later time point (Figure S5E). Western blot revealed increased levels of p300, CBP, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac at 48 hours following ML-60218 treatment (Figure S5F). However, despite continued elevation of p300 and CBP at 96 hours, levels of H3K27ac and H4K20ac declined. Importantly, NAT10 protein levels remained unchanged at both time points, suggesting that the observed effects are due to reduced levels of tRNAs rather than impaired NAT10 enzymatic activity. To test whether the upregulation of p300/CBP is not simply due to the transcriptional stress induced by Pol III inhibition, we examined RNA-seq data and found that Rn7sk, a Pol III-transcribed RNA was significantly upregulated in Nat10 KO cells (Figure S5G). However, chromatin accessibility and enhancer marks were unchanged at the Rn7SK locus (Figure S5H), suggesting that the increase in p300/CBP levels in Nat10 KO cells is not due to the transcriptional disruption induced by global Pol III inhibition.

To explore whether serine and leucine tRNAs interact with p300, we performed single-molecule RNA FISH to compare their nuclear localization with p300. Colocalization analysis revealed modest overlap between these tRNAs and p300, with tRNA-Leu showing slightly higher correlation (Figure 6K). In addition, p300 RNA-ChIP enriched both tRNAs from the caRNA fraction (Figure 6L), with Nat10 KO cells exhibiting increased association with tRNA-Ser variants and a trend towards decreased association with tRNA-Leu variants. To further assess the interaction between tRNAs and p300, we performed a pull-down assay with a recombinant catalytic subunit (amino acids 965–1810) of p300 and in vitro transcribed ac4C (+/−) variants of tRNA-Leu-CAG (Figure S6A). This demonstrated that the p300 catalytic domain binds more efficiently to ac4C-modified tRNA-Leu-CAG than to its unmodified counterpart (Figure S6B). Since p300 interacts with acetylated tRNAs, we also investigated whether p300 plays a role in tRNA acetylation. Both ac4C dot blot (Figure S6C) and immuno-northern blot (Figure S6D) analyses showed that inhibition of p300 does not affect the level of tRNA acetylation.

To validate the interaction of serine and leucine tRNAs with chromatin in 4T1 cells, we performed Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification followed by deep sequencing (ChIRP-seq)49 (Figure S6E). tRNA-Leu exhibited a higher number of peaks, distributed across 15,886 genic regions (FDR, < 0.01), while tRNA-Ser, was enriched in 13,771 genic regions (FDR, < 0.01). Approximately 25% of ChIRP-seq peaks for each tRNA located to promoter regions (Figure 6M), and 45% were intergenic, with some additional enrichment in intragenic regions. Motif analysis of the ChIRP-seq peaks revealed an association of tRNA-Ser with the KLF17-binding motif, whereas tRNA-Leu peaks were enriched for YY1-binding motifs (Figure 6N) suggesting a shared chromatin-binding pattern for these tRNAs and these zinc finger transcription factors. Analysis of signal enrichment around promoters (TSS ± 2kb) showed that while tRNA-Ser binding at promoters remained unchanged in Nat10 KO cells, tRNA-Leu binding was increased (Figure 6O). In contrast, when the analysis was limited to enhancer regions, both tRNAs showed reduced enrichment in Nat10 KO cells.

Overlap analysis revealed that tRNA-Ser and tRNA-Leu bound 9,846 genes in common (Figure S6F), while ~70% promoters with altered p300 enrichment also harbor one of these tRNAs (Figure S6G). Integration of the common tRNA-binding genes with RNA-seq data from Nat10 KO cells showed that approximately one-third of the differentially expressed genes were directly associated with these tRNAs. The intensity and distribution of the peaks within the altered genes showed moderate changes in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 6P), potentially reflecting the reduced ac4C modification of tRNA-Ser/Leu in Nat10 KO independently of their chromatin binding.

To test the potential for tRNAs to directly impact p300/CBP acetyltransferase activity, we performed HAT assay in presence of ac4C+/− tRNAs. In vitro transcribed ac4C+ tRNA-Leu-CAG had moderate stimulation of p300 HAT activity (Figure 6Q–R). However, increased addition of unacetylated-tRNA acted as a competitive inhibitor and significantly reduced HAT activity. This result suggests that p300/CBP activity could be differentially modulated by the presence of these ac4C+/− tRNAs in chromatin and suggests an important regulatory role in gene expression.

To investigate whether destabilization of ac4C-modified tRNAs affects p300/CBP levels and histone acetylation in the native chromatin context, we performed shRNA-mediated stable knockdown of tRNA-Ser-TGA and tRNA-Leu-CAG variants. 48-hours after knockdown of tRNA-Leu-CAG resulted in an approximately 60% reduction in ac4C signal, while tRNA-Ser-TGA knockdown caused only a modest, non-significant ~10% reduction (Figure S6H). However, by 72 hours, tRNA-Ser-TGA levels were reduced by ~40%, which recovered to physiological levels by 96 hours (Figure S6I). In contrast, the knockdown of tRNA-Leu-CAG was sustained at both 72 and 96 hours. tRNA-Leu-CAG knockdown led to upregulation of both p300 and CBP at 48 hours, whereas tRNA-Ser-TGA knockdown had no effect on these proteins at this time point (Figure 6S). Interestingly, levels of H3K27ac and H4K20ac were reduced in both knockdown conditions indicating that even modest disruption of ac4C-modified tRNAs destabilizes the chromatin environment. By 96 hours, p300 and CBP levels were also increased in tRNA-Ser-TGA knockdown cells (Figure S6J), but H3K27ac and H4K20ac levels remained unchanged probably due to the recovery of ac4C level at that time. In contrast, tRNA-Leu-CAG knockdown continued to show elevated p300/CBP levels along with reduced H3K27ac and H4K20ac. Taken together, our data imply that the ac4C modification status of specific tRNAs may serve as an important modulator of p300/CBP histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity.

NAT10 depletion affects transcription of chemokine and cytokine genes.

Since NAT10 depletion leads to altered p300 localization and disruption of enhancer organization, we next investigated whether NAT10 depletion also affects gene transcription. RNA-seq analysis revealed that NAT10 depletion had a robust effect on gene transcription. 783 genes were downregulated (Log2 FC), and 1410 genes were upregulated in Nat10 KO 4T1 cells (Figure 7A, Table S5). Many of the top downregulated genes were chemokines (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, Cxcl5, Cxcl10, Cxcl16, and Ccl2) and cytokine molecules (Il1a, Il23a, and Tgfb2), some of which (Cxcl1, Cxcl3, Ccl2, and Il1a) were previously identified as NUP210-dependent mechanosensitive genes8. Interestingly, downregulated genes (e.g., Nup210, Itgb1, Mmp9) were among the most highly expressed (Figure 7B). In contrast, silent or minimally expressed (Tnfrsf19, Scara5, Cdh17) were upregulated in Nat10 KO cells. Integration of RNA-seq data with H3K27ac ChIP-seq revealed that 248 genes showed concurrent decreases in both H3K27ac promoter signal and mRNA expression (Figure 7C, Table ST6A–B). Conversely, 217 genes exhibited increased H3K27ac promoter enrichment along with elevated expression. These findings suggest that transcriptional changes in a substantial subset of genes in Nat10 KO cells result directly from disrupted p300-dependent enhancer activity. GSEA hallmark pathway analysis revealed ‘MYC targets’ pathway emerged as one of the most significantly downregulated in Nat10 KO cells (Figure 7D), consistent with the reduced enhancer marks at the Myc super-enhancer region. Additionally, several immune-related pathways, including ‘inflammatory response’ and ‘TNFα signaling via NFκB’, were also downregulated. Consistently, reduced expression of chemokines and cytokines (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl16, Il1a), as well as Mmp9, in Nat10 KO cells, was accompanied by decreased enrichment of p300, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac, without notable changes in chromatin accessibility (Figure 7E). In contrast, typically silent genes such as Scara5 and Cdh17 showed an increased p300, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac enhancer marks, along with elevated chromatin accessibility and gene expression in Nat10 KO cells. This upregulation may result from the aberrant localization of p300 to developmentally silent heterochromatin regions following NAT10 loss. Taken together, these results suggest that NAT10 loss significantly affects p300/CBP-dependent transcriptional activity.

NAT10 depletion reshapes the innate immune tumor microenvironment and impairs neutrophil recruitment.

Since NAT10 loss suppresses the transcription of immune cell chemoattracting molecules, we sought to investigate whether Nat10 KO affects immune tumor microenvironment (TME) and thereby influence metastatic outcome. Implantation of 4T1 Nat10 knockdown cells into immunocompromised mice lacking T and B cells continued to suppress lung metastasis (Figure S7A–B), suggesting a mechanism independent of the adaptive immune response. Multiparametric flow cytometry revealed that 4T1 Nat10 KO tumors did not alter the total number of infiltrating leukocytes (CD45+) in the TME (Figure 7F). However, substantial changes in the distribution of immune populations were observed as shown by the unbiased t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE) projection of this single-cell data (Figure 7G). Unsupervised clustering followed by supervised cell annotation based on surface proteins expression (Table S7) identified T and B lymphocytes and the following innate immune cell populations: neutrophils (Neu), eosinophils (Eos), mononuclear phagocytes (MPs), and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) (Figure 7G). Nat10 KO tumors displayed significantly reduced neutrophil content, the most abundant immune cell type in 4T1 tumors, (Figure 7G–I; S7C–D), consistent with the observed decrease in the neutrophil-attracting chemokines (Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Cxcl5)39 (Figure 2N, 7A). We next focused on the second most abundant subset of innate immune cells, mononuclear phagocytes (MPs), which encompass monocytes (Mono), macrophages (Macs), and dendritic cells (DCs). The MP subset of cells was extracted, reclustered to define sub-populations, and visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Four clusters of Mono, five Macs, and four DCs were identified whose proportions were dysregulated in Nat10 KO resulting in an overall reduction of Mono and increase in Macs and DCs (Figure 7J–K, S7E–F). However, only total numbers of Mono were reduced by Nat10 depletion (Figure 7L, S7G–H). This reduction was driven mainly by the Mono1 subset, which resemble classical Ly6C+ monocytes commonly found in circulation and is consistent with the observed downregulation of their primary recruiting chemokine CCL240 (Figure 2N, 7A, S7G–H). Furthermore, analysis of Mac subsets divided into pro- or anti-tumorigenic phenotypes based on CD206 (pro-tumor associated) and MHCII (anti-tumor associated) expression showed an increase in the proportion of anti-tumor clusters Mac1 and Mac2 resulting in an overall reduction in the pro/anti-tumor Mac ratio in Nat10 KO tumors (Figure S7F–J). These results indicate that the loss of NAT10 suppresses metastasis in part by shaping the innate immune compartment of the primary tumor microenvironment. Supporting our observations, several studies have shown a role for neutrophils41–44 and monocytes/macrophages45,46 in driving 4T1 metastasis.

Taken together, our findings suggest that in response to tumor microenvironment stiffness, NAT10 upregulation promotes transcription of metastasis-associated genes, including chemokines and cytokines, through an acetylated tRNA–p300/CBP–dependent mechanism. These chemoattractants recruit neutrophils and monocytes, facilitating metastatic dissemination. Conversely, NAT10 depletion reduces acetylation of chromatin-associated tRNAs, leading to altered p300 localization, disrupted enhancer organization, and repression of genes required for both metastatic progression and myeloid cell recruitment, thereby creating a less metastasis-permissive microenvironment.

DISCUSSION:

In this study, we identify NAT10 as a metastasis-associated factor in breast cancer. Beyond its canonical nucleolar role, NAT10 is sensitive to extracellular matrix stiffness and interacts with NUP210 at the nuclear pore. Although the precise mechanistic link between NAT10 and NUP210 remains to be defined, our data indicate that they act within a shared pathway. NAT10 regulates transcriptional activity at the NUP210 enhancer, and a subset of NAT10 localizes to nuclear pore-associated enhancers enriched for NUP210. Loss of NAT10 phenocopies the adhesion defects and reduced metastatic potential observed in NUP210-deficient cells, supporting a common role in enhancer regulation at the nuclear periphery. Given that breast cancer cells disseminate early as the tumor microenvironment becomes hypoxic and fibrotic47, this pathway may facilitate metastatic spread. Together, these findings establish NAT10 as a key regulator of enhancer function and metastasis and underscore the need for further mechanistic investigation.

This study also highlights a previously unrecognized role of acetylated tRNAs in transcriptional regulation. tRNAs have been shown to play significant roles in various cancers 48, and recent studies have proposed mechanisms by which tRNA-derived fragments contribute to breast cancer 49,50. Our findings provide evidence that acetylated tRNAs particularly tRNA-Leu-CAG, act as modulators of p300/CBP function within chromatin. Knockdown of tRNA-Leu-CAG, but not tRNA-Ser-TGA, led to significant upregulation of p300/CBP and a corresponding paradoxical decrease in histone acetylation, highlighting a dominant role for tRNA-Leu-CAG in regulating this pathway. Supporting the functional relevance of this pathway, our recent data show that deletion of Thumpd1, a tRNA adaptor protein required for NAT10-mediated tRNA acetylation, also significantly reduces lung metastasis in a mouse model of breast cancer51. This further underscores the importance of the NAT10–tRNA acetylation axis in promoting metastatic progression and suggests that disrupting this pathway may impair key epigenetic mechanisms driving metastatic dissemination.

Additionally, NAT10 depletion leads to aberrant sequestration of p300 by nascent RNAs, likely due to the loss of protective interactions with acetylated tRNAs. Nascent transcripts can impede chromatin regulators from accessing nucleosomes37, while tRNAs can protect RNA binding proteins from promiscuous binding to other RNA species52. Absence of ac4C-modified tRNAs appears to disrupt this balance, resulting in stalled transcriptional complexes in which p300 becomes increasingly retained on nascent RNA rather than productively engaging with chromatin. Thus, the elevated association of p300 with nascent RNA in NAT10-deficient cells reflects a secondary consequence of abortive transcription and underscores the broader role of tRNA acetylation in safeguarding enhancer activity and transcriptional fidelity.

In conclusion, NAT10 is a mechanically sensitive regulator of metastasis in breast cancer functioning through its association with our previously described mechanically sensitive microenvironmental sensor mechanism that involves the nuclear pore protein, NUP210. NAT10’s impact on metastasis occurs through an unprecedented interaction with the histone acetyltransferase p300/CBP via tRNA acetylation, establishing pro-metastatic chromatin structure and transcriptional programs. Decreased tRNA acetylation by NAT10 loss leads to alteration of p300/CBP activity and the reorganization of chromatin architecture resulting in significant changes in transcription and cellular adhesion. Moreover, NAT10 loss led to reduced production of myeloid cell recruiting chemokines, which reduced metastasis-promoting neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages in the primary tumor. Further research will be required to extensively characterize the role of NAT10 in regulating tumor-immune crosstalk and metastatic progression.

Limitation of the study:

The in vitro transcribed tRNAs used in this study lack endogenous post-transcriptional modifications, which may influence their properties. These synthetic tRNAs also contain ac4C modifications at all cytosines rather than the native C12 site, potentially affecting structure and function. The direct role of ac4C-modified tRNAs in metastasis could not be tested due to the redundancy of tRNA gene copies, which precludes CRISPR-mediated editing of ac4C site. Moreover, metastasis assays with transfected tRNAs were not feasible because of the possibility of rapid dilution and degradation during tumor growth. Finally, direct evidence linking ac4C-modified chromatin-associated tRNAs to p300 chromatin occupancy remains to be demonstrated.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of the mouse Nat10 gene

To perform knockout of Nat10 in mouse 4T1 cells, the Cas9 D10A double-nicking strategy was employed. Sense (5′-TGGTGTTATAAGAAAGAGCT-3′) and antisense (5′-CCGAGCCTTCACAGTTGCCT-3′) sgRNAs spanning exon 3 of Nat10 were designed using the CRISPic web portal of the Broad Institute (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/gppx/crispick/public). The All-in-One plasmid, which encodes dual U6 promoter-driven sgRNAs and an EGFP-coupled Cas9 D10A plasmid (AIO-GFP), was provided by Dr. Steve Jackson (The Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute, UK) (Addgene #74119). The sgRNAs were inserted into the AIO-GFP plasmid using the method described previously53. In brief, the AIO-GFP plasmid was first digested with the BbsI restriction enzyme, dephosphorylated using calf intestinal phosphatase, and gel purified. sgRNA oligonucleotide pairs were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies, annealed, and phosphorylated with T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB). First, the sgRNA was cloned into the BbsI site, and second, the sgRNA was cloned into the BsaI site. The final clone’s DNA sequence was confirmed through Sanger sequencing using a specific primer (5′-CTTGATGTACTGCCAAGTGGGC-3′). The sgRNA plasmid was then transfected into 4T1 cells using Nanojuice transfection reagent (Millipore). After 48 hours, GFP-positive cells were sorted using flow cytometry, and single cells were plated on a 96-well plate. Cas9 D10A-edited clones were identified through PCR and Sanger sequencing of genomic DNA.

Protein lysate preparation and western blotting

Whole-cell protein lysate was prepared by utilizing lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 400 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 10 mM NaF, 1 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol, and a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. After addition of lysis buffer, lysates were sonicated in a Bioruptor Plus sonicator (Diagenode) for 3 cycles (30 sec on/30 sec off). Sonicated lysates were then incubated on ice for 20 minutes and cleared with centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes.

For the preparation of nuclear protein lysates, primarily used to isolate chromatin-bound proteins, the Nuclear Complex Co-IP Kit (Active Motif) was used according to manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, cells were harvested by scraping in 1x PBS containing a phosphatase inhibitor, then pelleted by centrifugation at 430 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1x hypotonic buffer to induce swelling and lysed with the addition of detergent. Nuclei were isolated by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 30 seconds. The nuclear pellet was lysed in complete digestion buffer, and chromatin was sheared using a micrococcal nuclease-based enzymatic shearing cocktail for 10 minutes at 37°C. The shearing reaction was halted by adding 0.5M EDTA, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant, containing the nuclear lysates, was collected, and protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 20–40 μg of protein lysates were combined with 4X NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Invitrogen) and 10X NuPAGE Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen), followed by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min. The resolved samples were run on either NuPAGE 3–8% Tris-acetate or NuPAGE 12% Bis-Tris protein gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the appropriate running buffer. Proteins were then transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore), and the membrane was blocked with blocking buffer (TBST + 5% non-fat dry milk) for 1 h. Subsequent steps involved overnight incubation with the relevant primary antibodies, washing with TBST, incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h, and development of the signal on X-ray film using the Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva).

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Immunofluorescence analysis was carried out as described previously 8. Briefly, cells were cultured on 4- or 8-well polymer coverslips (Ibidi) at seeding densities of 40,000 or 20,000 cells per well, respectively. After 24–48 h of incubation, cells underwent fixation with −20 °C methanol for 2 min and permeabilization with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% Triton X-100 for 1 min. Cells were then blocked with immunofluorescence buffer (1× PBS, 10 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.02% SDS, and 0.1% Triton X-100) for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in immunofluorescence buffer overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with immunofluorescence buffer (5 min per wash), the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional washes with immunofluorescence buffer, the cells were stained with 1 μg/ml DAPI for 10 min to label the nucleus. After three washes with PBS, slides were stored at 4 °C until subjected to confocal microscopy. In cases of drug treatment followed by super-resolution microscopy, cells were treated with 5–10 μM A-485, for 24 h. Images were captured using either a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan super-resolution microscope (Airyscan detector, ×63 plan-apochromat NA 1.4 oil-immersion objective lens, and 0.05 μm X–Y pixel size), or a Nikon SoRa spinning disk super-resolution microscope (Yokogawa SoRa spinning disk unit, ×60 plan-apochromat NA 1.49 oil-immersion objective lens, Photometrics BSI sCMOS camera, and 0.027 μm X–Y pixel size). Tetraspeck 0.2 μm beads (Invitrogen) were imaged with the same microscope parameters and used for channel alignment. Further image processing was carried out in Zeiss ZEN Blue V2 software.

In vitro transcription of ac4C-modified tRNA-Leu-CAG

tDNA template for in vitro transcription was synthesized using the protocol described elsewhere 54. T7-promoter containing overlapped forward and reverse tDNA oligos were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies. Oligos were as follows:

Forward: TAATACGACTCACTATAGTCAGGATGGCCGAGCGGTCTAAGGCGCTGCGTTCAGGTCGC

Reverse: TGGTGTCAGGAGTGGGATTCGAACCCACGCCTCCAGGGGAGACTGCGACCTGAACGCAGC

Oligos were annealed by heating at 95°C for 2 minutes, followed by cooling at 22°C for 3 minutes. To fill-in the 5’ overhangs and create blunt ends, DNA polymerase I, Large (Klenow) Fragment (New England Biolabs) was used. The annealed double-stranded tDNA template was then purified by adding an equal volume of crush and soak buffer (50 mM potassium acetate, 200 mM KCl, pH 7.5) and two volumes of phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1). The mixture was vortexed for 30 seconds and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The upper aqueous phase was precipitated with 2.7 volumes of 100% ethanol and incubated at −80°C for 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4°C. The pellet containing the purified tDNA was dissolved in nuclease-free water and used for in vitro transcription. In vitro transcription of ac4C-modified tRNAs was carried out using the HighYield T7 RNA Synthesis Kit with ac4CTP (Jena Bioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 μg of double-stranded tDNA template was used in a 20 μl reaction volume. To generate ac4C-modified tRNA, CTP was completely substituted with N4-acetyl CTP in the transcription reaction. After mixing all the components, the reaction was incubated at 37°C for 4 hours in a PCR cycler. Following transcription, template DNA was digested by adding 1 μl of TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The in vitro synthesized tRNAs were then gel-purified using the ZR Small RNA PAGE Recovery Kit (Zymo). Finally, ac4C incorporation in the tRNA was confirmed using the ac4C dot blot method described below.

Histone acetyl transferase (HAT) assay

Recombinant human p300 (full-length), p300 catalytic subunit, CBP catalytic subunit, KAT7, NAT10, histone variants (H3.1, H3.2, H3.3, H4), and recombinant polynucleosomes were purchased from Active Motif. Recombinant human NAT10 derived from 293T cells was obtained from OriGene Technologies. The standard 5× HAT assay buffer (250 mM Tris-base, pH 8.0, 50% glycerol, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 5 mM DTT) was purchased from Sigma. For each reaction, 250–500 ng of recombinant protein was resuspended in HAT buffer supplemented with 0.75 mM acetyl-CoA (Sigma) and 1 μl of 200 mM butyric acid (Sigma). The final reaction volume was adjusted to 20 μl with nuclease-free water and incubated at 30 °C for 30 minutes. For reactions involving in vitro–transcribed tRNAs, a modified HAT buffer was used to optimize performance. This buffer contained 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM sodium butyrate, 1× Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), and 0.4 U/μL murine RNase inhibitor (New England Biolabs), along with 1 nM p300 catalytic subunit, 0.4 μg histone H3.3, and 0.2 mM acetyl-CoA. In vitro–transcribed tRNAs were gel-purified and refolded prior to use by heating at 95 °C for 2 minutes, cooling to 22 °C for 3 minutes, and incubating at 37 °C for 5 minutes. HAT reactions were terminated by adding NuPAGE 4× LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and heating at 95 °C for 5 minutes. Samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by western blotting as described above.

ac4C sequencing analysis of 18S rRNA

For ac4C sequencing of the helix 45 of 18S rRNA, total RNA was isolated using the standard Trizol method. Cells were directly lysed in TriPure lysis reagent (Sigma). After adding 200 μl chloroform, lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15min. 450 μl of the upper layer containing the RNA was separated in a fresh tube. Then 450 μl isopropanol was added to the solution and stored at −20°C for 1 h to precipitate RNAs. Precipitates were isolated by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, and then pellets were washed with 500 μl of 75% ethanol followed by centrifugation for 5 min. Pellets containing the total RNA were then resuspended in DEPC-treated H2O and treated with DNase I. 1 μg total RNA was treated with sodium cyanoborohydride (100 mM in H2O) or vehicle (H2O) in a final reaction volume of 100 μl. Reactions were initiated by the addition of 1 M HCl to a final concentration of 100 mM and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped by adding 30 μL of 1 M tris−HCl pH 8.0. the reaction volume was adjusted to 200 μl with H2O and RNA was purified via ethanol precipitation. The pelleted RNA was dried using a Speed-vac, resuspended in deioned H2O, and quantified using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. For the reverse transcription reaction with the SuperScript III enzyme, ~500 pg of treated RNA were incubated with 4.0 pmole of the helix 45 reverse primer (5’-TAATGATCCTTCCGCAGGTTCACCTAC-3’) in 1X Superscript III buffer at 65 °C for 5 min and transferred to ice for 1 min to facilitate annealing. After annealing, reverse transcriptions were performed by adding 200 units of the SuperScript III enzyme, 5 mM DTT, 25 units of RNasin, 500 μM dNTPs (5 mM GTP, 10 mM CTP, ATP, and TTP) and incubating at 55 °C for 60 min. Reactions were quenched by increasing the temperature to 70 °C for 15 min. The cDNA products from the reactions and controls were directly used in PCR. PCR reactions contained 2 μl cDNA in 50 μl PCR reaction with Phusion Hot Start Flex (New England Biolabs). Reaction conditions: 1X supplied HF buffer, 2.5 pmol each forward (5’-CGTCGCTACTACCGATTGGATGG-3’) and reverse (5’-TAATGATCCTTCCGCAGGTTCACCTAC-3’) primers, 200 μM each dNTP, 2 units of Phusion Hot Start enzyme, 2 μl template (Thermocycling conditions: 67 °C annealing, 34 cycles). PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel, stained with SYBR safe, and visualized on a UV transilluminator at 302 nm. Bands of the desired size were excised from the gel. DNA was extracted using a gel extraction kit (Zymo) and submitted for Sanger sequencing (GeneWiz) using 5 μM of the forward PCR primer. Processed sequencing traces were viewed using SnapGene software. Peak height for each base was measured, and the percent misincorporation was determined using the equation: “percent misincorporation = (peak intensity of T)/ (sum of C and T base peaks) X 100%”. Misincorporation values were determined by subtracting the background water control misincorporation levels from that of the corresponding reactions.

ChIP-seq analysis

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the ChIP-IT Express Enzymatic Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kit (Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4 × 106 4T1 cells were seeded onto 15 cm tissue culture dishes and cultured for 48 hours. For ChIP of histone modifications, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 5 minutes at room temperature. For BRD4, NUP210, RRP1B, NAT10, and SIPA1 ChIP, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 minutes at room temperature. For p300 and H3K9me3 ChIP, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by washing with ice-cold PBS. Formaldehyde cross-linking reaction was then stopped using a glycine stop-fix solution. Cells were collected by scraping and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer and homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer. Enzymatic shearing of chromatin was performed for 10 min at 37°C. The shearing process was stopped with 0.5 M EDTA, and the chromatin was isolated through centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Each ChIP reaction was carried out using 25 μg of chromatin, along with 25 μl of Protein G magnetic beads and specific antibodies. The antibodies used included anti-rabbit H3K27ac (0.16 μg) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-rabbit H3K27me3 (0.51 μg) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-rabbit H4K20ac (5 μg) (Millipore-Sigma), anti-rabbit p300 (2.3 μg) (Abcam), anti-rabbit H3K9me3 (5 μg) (Abcam), anti-rabbit Brd4 (0.5 μg) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-rabbit Nup210 (2 μg) (Bethyl Laboratories), anti-rabbit Rrp1b (1 μg) (Millipore-Sigma), anti-rabbit HA (5 μg) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-rabbit V5 (1:40 dilution) (Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-rabbit SIPA (10 μg) (Abcam). Following an overnight incubation at 4 °C on a rotator, the beads were washed with ChIP buffers, and DNA was eluted using elution buffer. The eluted DNA was then subjected to reverse-cross linking and proteinase K treatment, with DNA quality assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer before ChIP-seq library preparation. The library was prepared using the SWIFT Accel-NGS 2s plus DNA Library Preparation Kit (Swift Biosciences), and the pooled samples were subsequently sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 SP platform. ChIP-seq data peak calling was performed using either the Sicer 55 algorithm with a window size of 200 and gap size of 200 or MACS256 narrow peak in Galaxy platform57. For the differential binding analysis, the Bioconductor package DiffBind was used after calling the peaks using the MACS2 56 algorithm. ChIP-seq plot profiles were created using deeptool2 58. Promoters were defined as regions spanning ±2 kb from the transcription start site (TSS). Enhancers were identified based on the presence of H3K27ac peaks, excluding regions within ±2 kb of the TSS.Average read count scores for p300, H3K27ac, and H4K20ac were calculated using bed files containing MACS2-called peaks and the multiBigwigSummary tool from deepTools2 on the Galaxy platform.

ATAC-seq:

Chromatin accessibility in 4T1 wild-type (WT) and Nat10 knockout (KO) cells was assessed using the ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours of incubation, cells were trypsinized, and 100,000 cells from each replicate were collected into 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tubes. Cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, washed with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 μl of ice-cold ATAC lysis buffer. Differential ATAC-seq peak enrichment was analyzed using Partek Flow software.

RNA-seq analysis

4T1 cells were grown in 6 cm tissue culture dishes, with a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per dish. Three biological replicates were used for each condition. Following 48 h of incubation, total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. To eliminate DNA contamination, on-column DNase treatment was applied using RNase-free DNase (Qiagen). For library preparation, the Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) was used, and the pooled samples were subjected sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina). Analysis of differential gene expression from the RNA-seq data was conducted using the Partek Flow software. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the differentially expressed genes was carried out using the GSEA software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Isolation of chromatin-associated RNA and RNA-seq

Chromatin-associated RNA was isolated using the RNA Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (RNA-ChIP) kit (Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4T1 cells were cultured in 15 cm dishes at a seeding density of 4 × 106 cells per dish. After 48 hours, the cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. The fixation was quenched by adding 1x Glycine Stop-Fix solution. Cells were then harvested by scraping, followed by centrifugation at 3,500 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C. For nuclei isolation, the cell pellets were lysed in 1 ml of ice-cold Complete Lysis Buffer for 30 minutes and then homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer with 50 strokes. The release of nuclei was confirmed under a brightfield microscope. The lysates were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the nuclear pellet was resuspended in Complete Shearing Buffer containing RNase inhibitors. Chromatin was sheared using a Bioruptor Plus sonicator (Diagenode) for 3 cycles (30 seconds on, 30 seconds off) at 4°C. The sheared chromatin samples were then centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant containing the chromatin fraction was transferred to fresh tubes. The chromatin fraction was subjected to DNase I digestion by incubating with DNA digestion buffer and DNase I. The digestion was stopped by adding 0.5 M EDTA, and the digested DNA was removed by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant containing DNase I-digested chromatin was then treated with proteinase K at 42°C for 1 hour and reverse cross-linked at 65°C for 1.5 hours. RNA was subsequently purified from the reverse cross-linked samples using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Millipore-Sigma), yielding chromatin-associated RNAs. The concentration of chromatin-associated RNA was determined using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. To assess the effect of DNase I treatment on RNA isolation, RNA was isolated directly from chromatin either with or without prior DNase I treatment, or DNase I treatment was performed after RNA isolation. For RNA-seq of chromatin-associated RNAs, DNase I–treated RNA was assessed for quality using a Bioanalyzer and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 platform using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep kit with Ribo-Zero Plus (ligation-based) and paired-end sequencing.

RNA-ChIP:

Chromatin was isolated using the RNA Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (RNA-ChIP) kit (Active Motif) as described above. The concentration of chromatin was determined by isolating RNA from an aliquot of the chromatin preparation. RNA-ChIP reactions were performed using three biological replicates for both WT and KO (c43) samples, with each reaction containing the equivalent of 6 μg of RNA from chromatin. Immunoprecipitation was conducted in a 150 μl reaction volume, consisting of 25 μl of pre-washed Protein G Magnetic Beads, RNA-IP buffer, RNase inhibitors, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, and 2 μg of rabbit p300 antibody (Abcam). The reaction mixture was incubated overnight on a rotator at 4°C. The beads were then washed four times with 200 μl of Complete RNA-ChIP Wash Buffer 1, followed by two washes with 200 μl of Complete RNA-ChIP Wash Buffer 2. RNA was eluted from the beads with 100 μl of Complete RNA-ChIP Elution Buffer by rotating on an end-to-end rotator for 15 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant containing the eluted RNA was transferred to a fresh tube after magnetic separation of the beads. Both the immunoprecipitated RNA and input RNA samples were treated with proteinase K and reverse cross-linked at 65°C for 1.5 hours. The RNA was then purified using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Millipore-Sigma). To remove residual DNA, the purified RNA was treated with DNase I, followed by further purification using the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 Kit (Zymo). A total RNA library was then prepared using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA-seq Library Prep Kit with RiboZero Plus. Samples were pooled and sequenced on NextSeq 2000 P2 using paired-end sequencing. The samples had 52–67 million pass filter reads with more than 94% of bases above the quality score of Q30. Reads of the samples were trimmed for adapters and low-quality bases using Cutadapt before alignment with the reference genome (mm10) and the annotated transcripts using STAR. The average mapping rate of all samples was 90%. The mapping statistics were calculated using Picard software. Library complexity was measured in terms of unique fragments in the mapped reads using Picard’s MarkDuplicate utility. Enrichment profiles of p300-interacting RNAs were generated using deepTools258 in Galaxy Platform. Enhancers were defined by H3K27ac peak enrichment excluding ±2 kb regions around the TSS. To analyze p300 enrichment on tRNA variants, normalized bigWig files from WT and Nat10 KO samples were used. Average enrichment scores for each tRNA sub-variant genomic region were calculated using the UCSC mm10 tRNA annotation bed file and the multiBigwigSummary tool.

3D DNA Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization (FISH) analysis

3D DNA FISH analysis was performed with minor modifications using the protocol described elsewhere 59. A BAC clone spanning the mouse Myc super-enhancer region (Clone ID: RP23–203H19) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The BAC clone was purified using the NucleoBond Xtra BAC kit (Takara Bio). Alexa Fluor 488-labeled probe was generated through Nick translation system using the Chromatide Alexa Fluor 488–5-dUTP kit (Invitrogen). Labeled probe was precipitated and denatured at 85°C for 5 min, then pre-annealed at 37°C for 1 h and stored in a freezer until use. 4T1 cells were grown on μ-slide 15-well angiogenesis polymer coverslip (Ibidi) for 48 h. Cells were then fixed in 2% formaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. After 3 rinses in 1x PBS, cells were permeabilized in ice-cold 0.4% Triton-X100 for 5 min, rinsed with PBS and then treated with 0.1 μg/μl RNase A (Sigma) for 1 h at 37°C. After 3 rinses in 1x PBS, cells were permeabilized with ice-cold 0.7% Triton-X100 + 0.1M HCl for 10 min. After 3 rinses in 1x PBS, cells were denatured in 1.9M HCl for 30 min at room temperature. After 3 rinses in PBS, cells were hybridized with labeled probes overnight at 37°C in a dark and humid chamber. Cells were washed 2 times with 2x SSC for 30 min in the dark (at 37°C and later at room temperature). Cells were then washed with 1x SSC for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with 1 μg/ml DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. After 3 washes with 1x PBS, coverslips were mounted using Slow Fade Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen) and imaged using a Nikon SoRA spinning disk confocal microscope.

tRNA FISH combined with immunofluorescence:

tRNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed using a modified single-molecule RNA FISH protocol as previously described60. Briefly, 4T1 cells were cultured on Ibidi 8-well polymer coverslips for 24 hours, then fixed with ice-cold (−20 °C) methanol for 5 minutes. Cells were permeabilized in 2× SSC containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes, followed by a 5-minute wash in 2× SSC with 10% formamide. Custom 5’-Cy3-labeled DNA probes targeting tRNA-Ser-TGA and tRNA-Leu-CAG were designed and synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). Probes were used at a final concentration of 1 μM in hybridization buffer containing 10% dextran sulfate, 2 mM vanadyl-ribonucleoside complex, 0.02% BSA, 1 mg/mL yeast tRNA, 2× SSC, and 10% formamide. Rabbit anti-p300 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:250) was included in the hybridization mix. After overnight hybridization at 37 °C, cells were washed with 2× SSC/10% formamide for 15 minutes. Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:200) was added in hybridization buffer and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by another 15-minute wash in 2× SSC/10% formamide. A final wash was performed in 2× SSC/10% formamide containing 1 μg/mL DAPI for 30 minutes. Samples were stored in 2× SSC and imaged using a Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan super-resolution confocal microscope.

Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification Sequencing (ChIRP-seq):

To assess the interaction of tRNAs with chromatin, ChIRP-seq was performed as previously described61 with minor modifications. Briefly, 5 million 4T1 cells were seeded per 15 cm dish and cultured for 48 hours. Cells were then trypsinized, and 20 million cells (in 20 ml volume) were cross-linked in PBS with 1% glutaraldehyde for 10 minutes at room temperature. The cross-linking reaction was quenched with 2 ml of 1.25 M glycine for 5 minutes. Cells were centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 minutes, washed with 20 ml of ice-cold PBS, and lysed in 500 μl of lysis buffer containing PMSF, Superase-In RNase inhibitor, protease inhibitor cocktail, and DTT. Lysates were sonicated in a pre-chilled Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode) for 6 hours at the highest setting, using a 30-second on/45-second off cycle. Samples were briefly vortexed every 30 minutes. A 5 μl aliquot was taken to assess chromatin shearing efficiency by genomic DNA extraction. Following sonication, lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing chromatin was collected and stored at −80 °C. Biotinylated oligonucleotide probes targeting tRNA-Ser-TGA, tRNA-Leu-CAG, and LacZ (negative control) were designed using Biosearch Technologies’ ChIRP probe designer. Due to the short length of tRNAs, only one unique probe per tRNA was generated. 3’-Biotin-TEG-conjugated probes were synthesized by IDT. Two independent biological replicates were processed for both WT and Nat10 KO conditions. For each sample, 500 μl of chromatin was hybridized overnight at 37 °C with 1 ml of hybridization buffer and 0.5 μl of 100 μM probe. Following hybridization, 50 μl of C1 magnetic beads were added and incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C with shaking. Beads were washed five times with ChIRP wash buffer and resuspended in 500 μl of wash buffer after the final wash. A 50 μl aliquot was used for RNA extraction and ChIRP validation by qRT-PCR. The remaining material was subjected to de-crosslinking and DNA purification using the ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research). ChIRP and input DNA samples were pooled and sequenced on the NovaSeq X Plus 1.5B platform using the IDT xGen 2S Plus DNA Library Prep Kit with paired-end sequencing. Peak calling for tRNA-Ser-TGA and tRNA-Leu-CAG was performed using MACS2 (narrow peak mode) with an FDR cutoff < 0.01 on the Galaxy platform57. Peak annotation was carried out using the ChIPseeker Bioconductor package62. Motif enrichment analysis was performed using MEME-ChIP63. Enrichment of ChIRP-seq signals at promoters (±1 kb from TSS) and enhancers (defined by H3K27ac peaks excluding ±2 kb from TSS) was analyzed using deepTools258. Overlap analysis between ChIRP-seq and RNA-seq data was performed using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), and genome browser visualization was done using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)64.

Response to ECM stiffness assay

To investigate the impact of extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness on tumor cells, we utilized polyacrylamide hydrogel-bound cell culture plates with varying elastic moduli to represent ECM stiffness. Specifically, 6-well plates with elastic moduli of 0.2 kPa (considered soft) was purchased from Matrigen. Regular 6-well tissue culture dishes were used to represent stiff matrices (elastic moduli is >GPa). Each well of the plate was treated with either 20 μg/ml fibronectin (Sigma) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C or 50 μg/ml Type I Collagen (Gibco) overnight. 4T1 cells were then seeded onto either the soft or stiff matrix at a seeding density of 100,000 cells per well. After 48 h of incubation, cells were collected for protein isolation.

High-parameter flow cytometric analysis of primary tumor-infiltrating leukocytes.

4T1 WT and Nat10 KO-c43 primary tumors were harvested from BALB/c mice 34 days post-implantation. To obtain single cell suspensions, tumors were mechanically disrupted and then enzymatically digested for 1h at 37°C, with 1mg/ml of DNase (Roche, 10104159001) and 200U of collagenase IV (GIBCO, 17104–019) in 0.5% FCS RPMI 1640 media. The digested tissue was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer followed by 3 min incubation with ACK lysing buffer at room temperature. The cell suspensions were then stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell Stain kit (Invitrogen) in PBS followed by incubation with antibody cocktail prepared in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, 563794). Fc receptors were blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody (2.4G2, BioXcell). For biotinylated antibodies, cells were subsequently incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated streptavidin (BD Biosciences). The following antibodies were used: CD11b (M1/70), CD44 (IM7), CD45 (30-F11), CD86 (GL1), CD24 (M1/69), Ly6G (1A8), CD64 (X54–5/7.1), SiglecH (440C), PD-L1 (MIH5), MHCII (I-A/I-E) (M5/114.15.2), CD135 (A2f10.1), Ly6C (AL-21) from BD Biosciences; CD103 (2E7), Nkp46 (29.A1.4), ICAM1 (YN1/1.7.4), SiglecF (S17007L), CD206 (C068C2), F4/80 (BM8), CD127 (A7R34) from Biolegend; CD11c (N418), CD3e (145–2C11), TCRb (H57–597), TCRgd (GL-3) from ThermoFisher scientific.

Samples were acquired using a FACS Symphony A5 flow cytometer equipped with BD FACSDIVA software (BD Biosciences). Absolute cell counts were quantified using CountBright™ Absolute Counting Beads (Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Acquired data were analyzed as previously described using FlowJo™ software (version 10.10) and R packages 65. Tumor infiltrating leukocytes were selected following this gating strategy in FlowJo™: debris were excluded using FSC-A versus SSC-A, doublets were excluded using FSC-H versus FSC-A, dead cells were removed using SSC-A versus Live/Dead positive cells, and tumor infiltrating leukocytes were selected using SSC-A versus CD45 positive cells. Asinh transformation, Phenograph clustering, T-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and Uniform Manifold Approximation Projection (UMAP) were ran combining all samples (15,000 cells/sample), using the R Spectre package 66. Clusters were annotated based on the expression level of surface markers (Table S7) and frequency of total leukocytes and each leukocyte population were extracted. Absolute number per mg of tumor was then calculated. ComplexHeatmap package 67 was used to represent the level of expression of each marker of interest.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis of cell-based and mouse model experiments

Statistical analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism 8 software. In vivo animal studies involved the calculation of p values using the Mann–Whitney U test for comparing two groups, and the outcomes were presented as mean ± standard deviation. In cases where more than two groups were compared, analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate multiple comparison tests was performed. Statistical comparison of microscopy image analysis was performed either through the Mann–Whitney U test for the comparison of two groups or ANOVA with appropriate multiple comparison tests for the comparison of multiple groups. For the analysis of qRT-PCR results, p values were determined using multiple two-tailed, unpaired t-tests, and the results were reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m). Details of statistical analyses are provided in the figure legends. Exact n values (number of animals or cells analyzed) are indicated in the figures or corresponding legends. Experiments were conducted in duplicate or triplicate at minimum. In some cases of in vivo mouse studies, a single iteration was performed to minimize the use of animals. The robustness of key findings was confirmed by employing three different immunocompetent mice backgrounds in the metastasis assay, ensuring validation across diverse biological systems.

Quantification and statistical analysis of microscopy data

Nuclei stained for p300 and H3K9me3-marked/H3K27me3-marked heterochromatin or SC35 were segmented using the deep-learning Cellpose Python-package 68. Nuclei were segmented using the pre-trained nuclei model, while p300 and other marked condensates were segmented using custom-trained models. Intensities and Mander’s colocalization coefficients were quantified using the scikit-image measure module 69. p-values were calculated using Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA with Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Myc superenhancer distances to heterochromatin foci were quantified using Imaris image analysis software (Bitplane). Nuclei were segmented using the Surfaces module based on DAPI fluorescence intensity. Heterochromatin foci were segmented using the Surfaces module based on high-DAPI fluorescence intensity and Myc enhancer FISH signals were segmented using the Spots module. The shortest distance between each Myc FISH spot and heterochromatin foci surface was calculated. p-values were calculated using Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA with Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Details of statistical analyses are provided in the figure legends. Exact n values (number of cells analyzed) are indicated in the figures.
