SUMMARY

Amplification of chromosomal material derived from 12q13–15 is common in human cancer and believed to result in overexpression of multiple collaborating oncogenes. To define the oncogenes involved, we overexpressed genes recurrently amplified in human liposarcoma using a zebrafish model of the disease. We found several genes whose overexpression collaborated with AKT in sarcomagenesis, including the tRNA methyltransferase METTL1. This was surprising, because AKT phosphorylates METTL1 to inactivate its enzymatic activity. Indeed, phosphomimetic S27D or catalytically dead alleles phenocopied the oncogenic activity of wild-type METTL1. We found that METTL1 binds the multi-tRNA synthetase complex, which contains many of the cellular aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and promotes tRNA aminoacylation, polysome formation, and protein synthesis independent of its methyltransferase activity. METTL1-amplified liposarcomas were hypersensitive to actinomycin D, a clinical inhibitor of ribosome biogenesis. We propose that METTL1 overexpression promotes sarcomagenesis by stimulating tRNA aminoacylation, protein synthesis, and tumor cell growth independent of its methyltransferase activity.

In brief

INTRODUCTION

Cancer genomes are shaped by mutational processes that change the sequence or structure of DNA, coupled to selection for mutations that increase fitness during tumor evolution.1 The identification of recurrent mutations affecting individual genes led to the discovery of many of the best-known oncogenes and tumor suppressors underlying malignant transformation.2–5 However, genetic lesions affecting multiple genes, such as aneuploidy or focal copy-number alterations of chromosomal segments, are highly prevalent in human cancer.6 Yet, for most of these aberrations, it remains unclear which of the genes involved drive selection during tumor evolution and which are so-called passenger genes affected only because of their physical proximity to the former. This distinction has important implications for the development of effective cancer therapeutics.

Amplification of chromosomal material derived from 12q13–15 is among the most common focal copy-number aberrations in human cancer.6 These lesions occur in multiple tumor types7–11 but are particularly common in well-differentiated and dedifferentiated liposarcoma.12–15 Well-differentiated and dedifferentiated liposarcomas are clonally related tumors on a biologic spectrum; dedifferentiated liposarcoma arises from well-differentiated liposarcoma and shows loss of adipocytic differentiation and more aggressive clinical behavior.15,16 For simplicity, we consider well-differentiated and dedifferentiated liposarcoma to be variants of the same disease, and we use the term liposarcoma to refer to these specific tumors hereafter.

We previously developed a zebrafish model of liposarcoma driven by a constitutively active AKT allele, which revealed that human liposarcomas are characterized by aberrant AKT activation.17 Here, we leveraged this zebrafish model to overexpress 12q13–15 genes recurrently amplified in human liposarcoma and found several that increase tumor penetrance. Among these genes, we were most surprised to find that overexpression of METTL1, which encodes a tRNA methyltransferase,18,19 collaborates with AKT in sarcomagenesis. Although work from our group and others has shown that the METTL1-catalyzed methylation of tRNAs promotes proliferation and tumor growth in several contexts,20–24 AKT phosphorylates METTL1 to inactivate its methyltransferase activity.25 We found that the oncogenic activity of METTL1 is independent of its methyltransferase activity. METTL1 binds to the multi-tRNA synthetase complex, a supramolecular complex that contains many of the cellular aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases,26 and METTL1 overexpression stimulates tRNA aminoacylation, polysome formation and protein synthesis independent of its methyltransferase activity.

RESULTS

The 12q13–15 amplicons harbor multiple oncogenes

To identify oncogenes within the characteristic 12q13–15 amplicons in human liposarcoma, we began by defining recurrently amplified genes. Our approach leveraged DNA copy-number analysis performed on a cohort of human 12q-amplified dedifferentiated liposarcomas as part of the Cancer Genome Atlas Sarcoma (TCGA-SARC) project.13 As previously described, these amplicons are derived from distinct regions of 12q (Figure 1A).12,27–29 Using thresholds for degree of gene amplification and recurrence across patient samples (see STAR Methods), we defined three minimal regions of 12q that are most consistently amplified in human liposarcoma: (1) a more centromeric 119-kb gene-rich region containing 11 protein-coding genes, including CDK4 (Figure 1B); (2) a middle 118-kb amplicon involving single gene, HMGA2 (Figure 1C); and (3) a more telomeric 1.5-Mb amplicon containing 12 protein-coding genes, including MDM2 (Figure 1D).

We previously developed a zebrafish model of liposarcoma, driven by a constitutively active myristoylated AKT2 allele (hereafter, AKT), in which liposarcomas develop at low penetrance in the tp53 wild-type context.17 To identify oncogenes within these minimal amplicons, we generated tp53 wild-type zebrafish overexpressing individual amplified genes, together with AKT, and tested whether these increased tumor penetrance. Transgene expression was driven by a rag2 promoter fragment that drives ectopic expression in mesenchymal tumor-initiating cells.17,30 Tumor onset was compared with that of control zebrafish overexpressing mCherry with AKT. We first focused on the only gene in the middle amplicon, HMGA2, because this is an established sarcoma oncogene.31 We found that HMGA2 overexpression increased the penetrance of AKT-induced sarcoma (Figure 1E), demonstrating that this model can identify known sarcoma oncogenes.

We then generated transgenic zebrafish overexpressing each of the protein-coding genes in the other minimal amplicons, which revealed several genes that collaborated with AKT in sarcomagenesis. In the most centromeric amplicon, these included CDK4, a well-established oncogene whose role in liposarcoma has long been suspected,32 as well as the tRNA methyltransferase METTL1, the transmembrane ubiquitin ligase MARCH9, and the phosphatase CTDSP2 (Figure 1F). Overexpression of CYP27B1, which catalyzes the rate-limiting step in vitamin D activation,33 showed a trend toward increasing tumor penetrance that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08; Figure 1F), and this result was virtually identical upon re-testing (Figure S1A). The six other genes within this most centromeric amplicon had no detectable oncogenic activity in the zebrafish model. In the most telomeric minimal amplicon, the penetrance of zebrafish liposarcoma was significantly increased by MDM2, also an established sarcoma oncogene,34 as well as the polyadenylation factor CPSF6, but none of the other tested genes (Figure 1G).

METTL1 overexpression drives sarcomagenesis

Among the genes that collaborated with AKT in sarcomagenesis in the zebrafish, we were most surprised to find the tRNA methyltransferase METTL1 (Figure 1F). Indeed, although recent work from our group and others has shown that the METTL1-catalyzed methylation of tRNAs promotes proliferation and tumor growth in several cellular contexts (reviewed in Cai et al.18 and Phizicky and Hopper19), AKT phosphorylates METTL1 to inactivate its enzymatic activity.25 As aberrant AKT activation is one of the most common events in human oncogenesis,35 these data suggested unexpected biology, thus we prioritized METTL1 for additional investigation. We cloned METTL1 cDNAs from human METTL1-amplified liposarcoma cell lines, and Sanger sequencing confirmed that the overexpressed transcript encodes the full-length wild-type protein (Uniprot: Q9UBP6-1). These data are in line with human sarcoma genomics, which have shown METTL1 gene amplification without mutations that alter its protein-coding sequence.12,13

Histologic analysis showed that the majority (71%) of tumors in zebrafish overexpressing METTL1 and AKT were infiltrative adipocytic neoplasms with variable cell size, nuclear pleomorphism and scattered lipoblasts, defined by their enlarged scalloped hyperchromatic nuclei and multivacuolated cytoplasm (Figure 1H). These pathologic findings are those of well-differentiated liposarcoma in humans.15 The remainder of tumors were rhabdomyomas, benign tumors with skeletal muscle differentiation (Figure S1B) that we have not previously observed in the zebrafish model of AKT-driven sarcoma. Using METTL1 immunohistochemistry with an antibody dilution designed to avoid giving detectable signal in normal tissues, we found that a subset of human tumors of skeletal and smooth muscle overexpress METTL1 (Figure S1C).

To test whether METTL1 has oncogenic activity in human liposarcoma, we asked whether its expression is selectively required for the survival of METTL1-amplified human liposarcoma cells. Using quantitative DNA PCR in the four 12q-amplified liposarcoma cell lines available to us, we found that three of the four had high-level amplification of METTL1 (LP6, T449, LPS853), whereas LPS141 cells lacked such METTL1 amplification; copy-number analysis of MDM2 was used to confirm that all four have 12q amplification (Figure 1I). We found that METTL1 knockdown was selectively toxic to human liposarcoma cells with high-level amplification of METTL1, and not to LPS141 cells (Figure 1J). We confirmed that toxicity of the METTL1-targeting shRNA was on-target because transduction of a METTL1 cDNA that escapes shRNA targeting rescued viability (Figure 1K).

The role of METTL1 in sarcomagenesis is methyltransferase independent

We then turned our attention to the interaction between METTL1 and AKT. In the zebrafish model, METTL1 overexpression was much more oncogenic in collaboration with AKT than MDM2 (Figure 2A), suggesting a specific interaction between the METTL1 and AKT oncoproteins. AKT has previously been shown to phosphorylate serine 27 of METTL1 in response to growth factor stimulation to inhibit its tRNA methyltransferase activity,25 a regulatory modification with undefined cellular consequences.19 Consistent with this, we found that transduction of mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) with the myristoylated AKT allele increased S27 phosphorylation of wild-type METTL1 but not a non-phosphorylatable S27A mutant (Figure 2B). Western blot analysis of cells transduced with AKT and a catalytically dead (L160A/D163A) METTL1 allele (hereafter, METTL1-CD) suggested that this allele could also be phosphorylated by AKT (Figure S3A).

To validate the previous report that METTL1 phosphorylation on S27 impairs its enzymatic activity,25 we generated recombinant wild-type, phosphomimetic S27D, non-phosphorylatable S27A, and catalytically dead (CD) (L160A/D163A) METTL1 proteins in complex with WDR4, which is its obligate cofactor for tRNA methylation.36,37 We then tested the methyltransferase activity of the resultant complexes on a tRNA substrate in a cell-free biochemical assay. We found that the METTL1-S27D allele that mimics AKT-induced phosphorylation had severely impaired tRNA methyltransferase activity, whereas the enzymatic activity of the S27A non-phosphorylatable mutant was indistinguishable from wild type (Figure 2C). Mutating G46 on the tRNA substrate abolished all methylation (Figure 2C), indicating that this assay reflected canonical tRNA-G46 methylation by the METTL1-WDR4 complex.

The above data raised the surprising possibility that the role of METTL1 in sarcomagenesis might be methyltransferase independent. To test this directly, we overexpressed the METTL1 alleles described above in the zebrafish model and asked whether these increased the penetrance of AKT-induced liposarcoma. We found that the CD and the phosphomimetic methyltransferase-impaired S27D alleles of METTL1 had oncogenic activity that was at least as potent as that of wild-type METTL1 (Figures 2D and 2E). By contrast, the oncogenic activity of the non-phosphorylatable S27A mutant was impaired (Figure 2E), even though its methyltransferase activity is intact (Figure 2C). Overexpression of METTL1-S27D with mCherry control was sufficient to induce sarcomas at low penetrance, but this was significantly less oncogenic than the combination of METTL1-S27D with AKT (Figure 2E), indicating that METTL1 is not the only relevant target of AKT during sarcomagenesis.

To test the relevance of these findings to mammalian oncogenesis, we tested the ability of wild-type METTL1 and its two S27 alleles to impart colony-forming activity to MEFs. Colony forming activity was induced by overexpression of either wild-type METTL1 or the phosphomimetic S27D allele but only when these were co-transduced with activated AKT (Figure 2F). The inability of wild-type METTL1 to induce clonogenic activity without AKT in this experiment contrasted with our prior data showing that the co-overexpression of METTL1 with WDR4, its obligate cofactor for tRNA methylation, is clonogenic in this assay.20 To test how WDR4 and AKT interact with METTL1 in sarcomagenesis, we generated transgenic zebrafish co-overexpressing these three genes. We found that WDR4 overexpression suppressed the ability of either wild-type or CD METTL1 to increase the penetrance of AKT-driven sarcoma (Figures S2A and S2B). We also found that WDR4 mRNA expression was low in liposarcoma when compared with all other tumors analyzed in DepMap (Figure S2C), and WDR4 protein levels were low in liposarcoma cells compared with 293T controls (Figure S2D), indicating that METTL1 amplification is not accompanied by co-overexpression of WDR4 in human liposarcoma. We considered the possibility that WDR4 overexpression might suppress the oncogenic activity of METTL1 by impairing its ability to be phosphorylated by AKT. However, we found that overexpression of WDR4 increased the stability of both total and S27-phosphorylated METTL1 (Figure S2E), suggesting that suppression of oncogenesis by WDR4 overexpression is explained by alternative mechanisms that remain to be defined. These data provide further support to the conclusion that the role of METTL1 in sarcomagenesis is independent of its ability to methylate tRNAs.

To test whether the requirement of METTL1 for tumor maintenance in human liposarcoma is also methyltransferase independent, we tested whether the toxicity of METTL1 knockdown to human METTL1-amplified liposarcoma cells could be rescued by expression of cDNAs encoding distinct METTL1 alleles. We found that expression of either the methyltransferase-defective S27D or the CD L160A/D163A alleles of METTL1 rescued the toxicity of METTL1 knockdown as well as wild type (Figure 2G). By contrast, the non-phosphorylatable and methyltransferase-competent S27A allele was unable to rescue the toxicity of METTL1 knockdown, despite similar levels of protein expression (Figures 2G and S3B). Of note, although overexpression of the METTL1 S27D mutant had stronger oncogenic activity than wild-type METTL1 in the zebrafish model (Figure 2E), this was not the case in mammalian systems (Figures 2F and 2G), so this finding was not further investigated. Thus, the role of METTL1 in sarcomagenesis is independent of its methyltransferase activity.

METTL1 promotes polysome formation and protein synthesis

To our knowledge, METTL1 has no known methyltransferase-independent functions. To search for noncanonical functions of this protein, we began by assessing its subcellular localization in METTL1-amplified liposarcoma cells. Using subcellular fractionation by centrifugation, we found that the METTL1 protein was particularly abundant in the crude ribosomal fraction (P100) (Figure 3A), raising the possibility that METTL1 might regulate ribosomal function independent of its methyltransferase activity. To test this, we began with polysome profiling, a technique that sub-fractionates ribosomes into individual 40S or 60S subunits, 80S monosomes, and polysomes, which are multiple ribosomes translating the same mRNA. We first assessed effects of overexpression of the constitutively active AKT allele in MEFs and found that this stimulated formation of both monosomes and polysomes (Figure S4), consistent with its established role as a positive regulator of translation initiation.38 Co-transduction of the phosphomimetic and catalytically defective METTL1-S27D allele with AKT increased polysome formation when compared with EGFP + AKT control, and the non-phosphorylatable but catalytically intact METTL1-S27A + AKT failed to phenocopy this effect (Figure 3B). In METTL1-amplified liposarcoma cells, shRNA knockdown of METTL1 decreased polysome formation (Figure 3C).

The increase in polysome formation triggered by METTL1 overexpression could reflect increased protein synthesis or, alternatively, an impairment in translation elongation, leading to an accumulation of stalled ribosomes.39 To distinguish these possibilities, we used a radiolabeled phenylalanine incorporation assay to measure the rate of protein synthesis. We found that transduction of either wild-type or CD METTL1 alone stimulated translation at least as much as constitutively active AKT, and co-expressing either of these METTL1 alleles with AKT had a greater effect than either alone (Figure 3D). The effects of METTL1 and AKT on phenylalanine incorporation reflected stimulation of translation because they were blocked by the translation inhibitor cycloheximide (Figures 3D and 3E).40 The ability of wild-type METTL1 to stimulate translation was phenocopied by the phosphomimetic and catalytically defective METTL1 S27D mutant, whereas overexpression of the nonphosphorylatable S27A allele, which is enzymatically competent, failed to increase phenylalanine incorporation (Figure 3E).

An increase in polysome formation and protein synthesis could reflect the direct effects of METTL1 on the cellular translation machinery or be secondary to other potential consequences of METTL1 overexpression (e.g., increased proliferation). To test whether METTL1 regulates translation directly, we leveraged the rabbit reticulocyte lysate system to assess effects on translation in lysates derived from these cells, which lack nuclei/nucleoli and are therefore unable to support RNA or ribosome biogenesis. Rabbit reticulocyte lysates were incubated with recombinant METTL1 or AKT proteins as well as a reporter luciferase mRNA, and translation efficiency was assessed by luminescence. We found that the addition of recombinant METTL1 and AKT each individually increased translation in this assay, and both proteins combined had a significantly greater effect than either one alone (Figure 3F). This effect was phenocopied by the METTL1-S27D mutant, whereas the METTL1-S27A mutant had a statistically significant impairment in this assay of modest effect size (Figure S5A). The modest impairment of the S27A mutant in this in vitro translation assay contrasts with its profound impairment in cellular assays for translation (i.e., Figure 3E), suggesting that the mechanisms through which the S27A mutant is impaired in regulation of cellular translation are not fully captured by this in vitro translation assay.

If METTL1 is a sarcoma oncogene because it increases polysome formation and protein synthesis, we reasoned that METTL1-amplified tumor cells might be hypersensitive to small molecule inhibitors of ribosome biogenesis. To test this prediction, we leveraged actinomycin D, a clinical drug that functions as a selective inhibitor of RNA polymerase I at low nanomolar doses, thus blocking ribosome biogenesis by preventing transcription of ribosomal RNAs.41 Of note, actinomycin D becomes a global RNA synthesis inhibitor at higher doses, so we focused on low doses at which it exhibits selectivity for ribosomal RNAs. Ribosomal RNA levels were higher in METTL1-amplified LPS853 than in LPS141 cells lacking such amplification (Figure S5B), and we found that low nanomolar doses of actinomycin D were highly toxic to liposarcoma cell lines with high-level METTL1 amplification but much less so to LPS141 liposarcoma cells lacking such amplification or to normal human white preadipocytes (Figures 3G and S5C).

METTL1 binds the multi-tRNA synthetase complex and regulates tRNA aminoacylation

To test how METTL1 regulates translation, we first tested whether METTL1 localizes with ribosomal complexes. Although we initially linked METTL1 to translation based on its presence in a crude ribosomal fraction (Figure 3A), these fractions are not pure ribosomes but also contain auxiliary components of the translation machinery and several other factors.42 To assess whether METTL1 localizes with ribosomal complexes, we performed western blot analysis of ribosomal fractions collected during polysome profiling, which showed that all detectable METTL1 protein was in the free fraction and not bound to ribosomal complexes (Figure 4A). Thus, METTL1 regulates a cellular process required for protein synthesis in cells and in reticulocyte lysates but not at the ribosome. The data earlier in this manuscript showed that, in AKT-driven tumors, METTL1 stimulates translation independent of its ability to methylate tRNAs. However, we wondered whether this effect could still involve tRNAs because recent studies have shown that METTL1 contributes most of the tRNA binding interface of the METTL1-WDR4 heterodimer, and METTL1 can bind tRNAs as a monomer, albeit at low affinity compared with the METTL1-WDR4 heterodimer.43,44 Nevertheless, such a low-affinity interaction might be physiologically relevant, particularly in METTL1-amplified cancer cells.

Does METTL1 regulate tRNA aminoacylation? After tRNAs unload their amino acid onto a nascent protein at the ribosome, they are released as deacylated tRNAs that must be re-“charged” with their cognate amino acid by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (aaRS) to support ongoing translation.45,46 tRNA aminoacylation does not occur at the ribosome, providing a potential link between METTL1 and a nonribosomal cellular process required for translation. To test whether METTL1 overexpression promotes tRNA aminoacylation, we began by asking whether METTL1 knockdown in liposarcoma cells activates the stress-response kinase GCN2, which is activated by uncharged tRNAs to phosphorylate EIF2α (encoded by EIF2S1).47 We found that knockdown of METTL1 increased phosphorylation of both GCN2 and EIF2α in multiple METTL1-amplified liposarcoma cells but not in LPS141 cells lacking METTL1 amplification (Figure 4B). Knockdown of METTL1 in two additional sarcoma cell lines lacking METTL1 amplification also revealed little effect on GCN2 phosphorylation (Figures S6A–S6C), arguing that this function of METTL1 is particularly relevant in cells with METTL1 gene amplification.

We then asked whether METTL1 binds cellular aaRSs. In higher eukaryotes, 9 of the 20 cytoplasmic aaRSs assemble with three nonenzymatic proteins (AIMP1–3) in a supramolecular complex termed the multi-tRNA synthetase complex (MSC).48 This complex is believed to have evolved to promote the channeling of tRNAs between the cellular machineries for aminoacylation and translation.48–51 To test whether METTL1 binds the MSC complex, we overexpressed FLAG-tagged METTL1, FLAG-METTL1-CD, or FLAG-EGFP control in human liposarcoma cells and performed FLAG immunoprecipitation (IP)-western blot analysis after documenting performance of the anti-bodies for MSC components (Figure S7). We found that METTL1 pulled down its known cofactor WDR4, providing a positive control for the IP, as well as multiple components of the MSC (Figure 4C). To validate this finding, we performed the reciprocal IP of endogenous AIMP2, a scaffolding component of the MSC complex. We found that AIMP2 pulled down endogenous METTL1 in addition to multiple MSC components (Figure 4D). Thus, METTL1 binds the MSC. We also noted that WDR4 was pulled down much more robustly by IP of METTL1 than by IP of the MSC scaffolding subunit AIMP2 (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting that the METTL1-WDR4 and METTL1-MSC complexes may be distinct. To assess whether the localization of METTL1 to the MSC complex is selective to METTL1-amplified cells, we compared the ability of endogenous AIMP2 to pull down METTL1 in METTL1-amplified LPS853 cells vs. LPS141 cells lacking METTL1 amplification. We confirmed that AIMP2 robustly pulled down METTL1 in LPS853 cells but not in LPS141 cells. This is even though AIMP2 pulled down two other MSC components (AIMP2 and MARS) from LPS141 cells, suggesting that the MSC is present in these cells (Figure S6D).

We then asked whether METTL1 regulates tRNA aminoacylation (“charging”), leveraging a quantitative PCR-based assay to detect total and charged tRNA levels.52,53 The human genome is estimated to encode more than 600 tRNA genes,54 but we selected a representative subset of these for analysis. This included several tRNAs that are charged by aaRSs known to be present in the MSC and two tRNAs whose aaRS are not known MSC components (encoding valine and histidine tRNAs). We first assessed effects of METTL1 knockdown on total tRNA levels in human liposarcoma cells because knockdown impairs all functions of METTL1 and we previously showed that METTL1 regulates stability of multiple tRNAs in a methyltransferase-dependent manner.20 In line with our previous data, we found that METTL1 knockdown decreased the total abundance of multiple tRNAs, and this effect was rescued by restoring wildtype METTL1 but not its CD allele (Figure S8). We then turned our attention to regulation of tRNA aminoacylation by METTL1. We found that METTL1 knockdown impaired the charging of all tRNAs measured, and tRNA aminoacylation was rescued by re-expressing either wild-type or CD METTL1 (Figure 4E). This effect was phenocopied by the METTL1-S27D mutant that mimics AKT-induced phosphorylation, and not by METTL1-S27A (Figure S9A). Thus, METTL1 promotes tRNA aminoacylation independent of its methyltransferase activity.

Given our data indicating that METTL1 binds the MSC, we expected that METTL1 would regulate aminoacylation of tRNAs whose aaRSs are a known component of the MSC. However, we were initially surprised to find that METTL1 also promoted aminoacylation of Val-MAC and His-GTG, tRNAs whose aaRS are not known MSC components.48 However, western blot analysis of AIMP2 pulldowns revealed that both of these aaRSs, VARS and HARS, are in fact part of the MSC complex in METTL1-amplified liposarcoma (Figure 4D).

We then asked whether overexpression of METTL1, alone or in combination with constitutively active AKT, stimulates tRNA aminoacylation in MEFs, which do not overexpress these oncoproteins at baseline. We found that transduction of activated AKT alone (with dTomato control) decreased charging of all tRNAs measured (Figure 4F), consistent with the established role of AKT in stimulating translation initiation, and thus consumption of charged tRNAs. Overexpression of wild-type or CD METTL1 (without AKT) increased the charging of several tRNAs above levels in control cells. Moreover, the co-transduction of either wild-type or CD METTL1 with AKT not only reversed the decrease in tRNA charging induced by AKT but also increased tRNA aminoacylation above their basal levels for most tRNAs assessed (Figure 4F). This effect was phenocopied by the METTL1-S27D mutant and not METTL1-S27A (Figure S9B). These data support a model in which activation of AKT, a well-established positive regulator of translation initiation,35,55 stimulates increased initiation of translation and thus consumption of charged tRNAs; in this context, cells are unable to maintain tRNA charging at steady-state levels, and the availability of charged tRNAs becomes limiting for translation efficiency. Overexpression of METTL1 provides a mechanism to increase tRNA charging and relieve this limitation to further increase protein synthesis (Figure 5).

The above data indicated that overexpression of methyltransferase-dead METTL1 stimulates aminoacylation of all tRNAs analyzed, suggesting that METTL1 was likely to regulate global mRNA translation. However, these data did not exclude the possibility that transcript-selective effects might also be prominent. To investigate this possibility, we applied ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq), RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and proteomics analysis to MEFs transduced with pairs of transgenes: EGFP + dTomato, EGFP + AKT, METTL1-CD + dTomato, and METTL1-CD + AKT. We assessed the effects of AKT via pairwise comparisons of MEFs transduced with EGFP + dTomato vs. EGFP + AKT, and effects of METTL1-CD were assessed by comparing EGFP + AKT vs. METTL1-CD + AKT. As shown earlier in this manuscript, AKT and METTL1-CD each increase the total rate of protein synthesis in these specific comparisons (Figure 3D). We first focused on RNA-seq analysis and confirmed that upregulation of AKT and METTL1 mRNA was detectable in the appropriate comparisons (Figure S10A). We also noted that AKT2 had significant effects on global mRNA expression, consistent with its ability to regulate some transcription factors,35 whereas the effects of METTL1-CD on mRNA expression were modest (Figure S10A; Table S1). We then turned our attention to translation efficiency, which was assessed based on the ribosome occupancy of each mRNA transcript normalized for its expression level. AKT2 had modest effects on global translation efficiency in these cells, but it did notably downregulate its own translation, possibly reflecting an autoregulatory feedback loop (Figure S10B; Table S2). Transduction of METTL1-CD affected the translation efficiency of only 6 mRNAs, all with false discovery rates near the threshold of statistical significance, and none were obvious mediators of oncogenesis (Figure S10B; Table S2). Finally, we turned to proteomics analysis to assess relative changes in abundance of specific proteins in these cells. AKT transduction altered the level of several proteins (Figure S10C; Table S3). In the case of transduction with METTL1-CD, only NPR3, which is also not an obvious oncogenic factor, increased modestly just above the threshold of statistical significance (Figure S10C; Table S3). Although we cannot rule out the possibility that METTL1 has some transcript-selective effects below the limit of detection of these assays, these are not prominent effects. These data thus support a model in which the methyltransferase-independent oncogenic function of METTL1 is mediated by global stimulation of tRNA aminoacylation, protein synthesis, and tumor cell growth.

DISCUSSION

The oncogenes driving selection for some of the most common copy-number aberrations in human cancer remain incompletely defined. Here, we used a zebrafish model to define oncogenes within 12q13–15 amplicons, a common aberration in multiple tumor types, including liposarcoma.6,12,32 We confirmed the long-suspected roles of CDK4, MDM2, and HMGA2 as bona fide liposarcoma oncogenes and also found genes whose contribution to oncogenesis was not previously recognized. We were most surprised to find that overexpression of METTL1 collaborates with AKT activation in sarcomagenesis because AKT phosphorylates and inactivates the enzymatic activity of METTL1.25 Indeed, we found that the oncogenic function of METTL1 in zebrafish and human liposarcoma is independent of its methyltransferase activity. We found that METTL1 binds the multi tRNA synthetase complex and stimulates tRNA aminoacylation, polysome formation, and protein synthesis.

Under basal conditions, translation initiation is believed to be the rate-limiting step for protein synthesis, and most established mechanisms of translation regulation in normal and malignant cells function at the initiation step.38,56 However, our data support the model that, upon robust stimulation of translation initiation by AKT activation, tRNA aminoacylation becomes limiting to translation and oncogenic transformation. Amplification of METTL1 allows cells to overcome this constraint by restoring tRNA aminoacylation, thus allowing METTL1 and AKT to cooperate in oncogenesis by maximizing the rate of protein synthesis and tumor cell growth. Given that METTL1 binds the MSC, and this complex appears to have evolved to promote the channeling of tRNAs between the ribosome and aaRSs,48–51 it is tempting to speculate that METTL1 overexpression further facilitates channeling efficiency to promote the recharging of tRNAs and maintain translation efficiency.

Radiation and chemotherapy have modest activity in liposarcoma, and these tumors are largely incurable when complete surgical resection is impossible.57–60 The 12q amplicons have long been recognized to contain MDM2 and CDK4,8,34 thus liposarcoma was prioritized in clinical trials testing small molecule inhibitors of these proteins. However, although such inhibitors can slow disease progression, objective responses are rare,61–66 even when MDM2 and CDK4 inhibitors are combined.67 This raises the possibility that the 12q13–15 amplicons contain additional oncogenes whose therapeutic targeting will be important as part of effective combinations. Our findings implicate METTL1 as an additional oncogene whose activity is required for tumor maintenance. Surprisingly, this function is methyltransferase independent and is thus not amenable to therapeutic targeting using enzymatic inhibitors, at least in sarcomas with aberrant AKT activation. Our studies revealed that METTL1-amplified liposarcomas are hypersensitive to the ribosome biogenesis inhibitor actinomycin D, providing an alternative therapeutic strategy that should be tested as part of therapeutic combinations. Our finding that continuous exposure to low nanomolar doses of actinomycin D leads to sustained and selective killing of METTL1-amplified cells would support the selection of dosing regimens leading to drug exposure over several days as part of future clinical trials testing this approach. Given that activation of AKT signaling is one of the most common events in human oncogenesis,35 and that AKT phosphorylates and inactivates the methyltransferase activity of METTL1, it may be important to target nonenzymatic oncogenic functions of METTL1 in multiple tumor types.

Limitations of the study

Our initial screen for 12q-amplified genes that promote sarcomagenesis involved overexpressing human genes in a zebrafish model driven by a constitutively active AKT transgene. Thus, it is possible that additional oncogenes exist that were missed because the human protein failed to engage its relevant target in zebrafish or because their oncogenic function is the activation of AKT. A relatively high fraction of genes tested in the zebrafish model increased the penetrance of sarcoma. We favor the view that this reflects selection of these genes based on fairly stringent criteria for recurrent amplification in human cancer, presumably reflecting positive selection during human oncogenesis. However, we cannot rule out the alternative possibility that the zebrafish model could be more permissive to oncogenic transformation than human cells, and it is possible that some genes with oncogenic activity in the zebrafish model may not drive human sarcomagenesis. Our studies on METTL1 focused exclusively on the role of this protein in the context of human tumors with high-level METTL1 amplification, which harbor hundreds of METTL1 copies per cell, or in MEFs overexpressing exogenous METTL1. Moreover, the tRNA affinity of METTL1 is low when it is not bound to its cofactor WDR4.43 Our findings could be used to support an argument that the ability of METTL1 to localize to the MSC and stimulate tRNA aminoacylation is unique to cells with METTL1 gene amplification. However, this function of METTL1 clearly did not evolve because it promotes tumor formation, and we believe it likely promotes fitness of normal cells under some conditions. However, our efforts to date have yet to reveal such conditions, whose definition will require additional investigation. Finally, although actinomycin D is a clinical drug, we would support testing this in patients with METTL1-amplified liposarcoma only in the context of a clinical trial.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Zebrafish transgenesis and tumorigenesis assays

Zebrafish of the AB wild-type strain were maintained under standard husbandry conditions.68 Zebrafish expression plasmids (30 pg of each), which encoded the cDNA sequence of interest driven by the zebrafish rag2 promoter, as well as a cardiac EGFP reporter cassette, both flanked by recognition sequences for the I-SceI meganuclease to promote efficiency of transgene integration, were microinjected into wild-type AB-strain zebrafish embryos at the one-cell stage in a solution containing 30 ng/μl of each plasmid and 0.3 U/μl I-SceI (R0694, New England Biolabs) in NEB rCutSmart buffer, as previously described.17 At 2 days post-fertilization (dpf), cardiac EGFP fluorescence was used to isolate zebrafish with successful transgene integration, which were then followed weekly for the development of visible tumors for 180 days.

METHOD DETAILS

Defining minimal amplicons in 12q-amplified human liposarcoma

We assessed DNA copy number in human 12q-amplified liposarcomas by leveraging single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays performed on a cohort of 50 dedifferentiated liposarcoma clinical specimens by TCGA-SARC,12,13 available in NCBI GEO under accession number GSE21124. Six of these 50 cases lacked evidence of 12q amplification, and so were excluded from our analysis (cases excluded: PT1DD, PT13DD, PT20DD, PT21DD, PT24DD, PT47DD). Minimal amplicons were defined as a region of genomic amplification, defined as log2 DNA copy number ratio ≥ 1, in probes spanning at least 100 kb in genomic region, and this amplified region occurring in at least 70% of cases analyzed. Note that when a single SNP array probe within a 100 kb region showed log2 copy number ratio less than 1, but all other probes were consistent with amplification, this was considered an outlier probe and the amplification call was still made. Heatmaps depicting DNA copy number were generated using Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/).

Zebrafish cDNA expression constructs

Expression constructs for each of the candidate genes were generated sequentially in two steps using the rag2:myr-mAKT2 plasmid, which was previously described.17 First, a cmlc2:EGFP selection marker was cloned in to allow zebrafish with successful expression of the transgenic cassette to be selected by cardiac EGFP fluorescence at 2 days post-fertilization (dpf). This was performed by first PCR amplifying cmlc2:EGFP from Addgene plasmid pDestTol2CG2-U6:gRNA (addgene #63156), and this was cloned into the MfeI restriction site of the rag2:myr-mAKT2 plasmid by digesting this plasmid with MfeI restriction enzyme (New England BioLabs), gel purification and blunt end ligation. The resultant plasmid was termed rag2:myr-AKT_CE_pISceI, with CE denoting cmlc2:EGFP.

Each of the candidate genes were subsequently cloned into this plasmid. cDNAs encoding the candidate liposarcoma genes were amplified from RNA extracted from the LP6 or T449 LPS cell lines using Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen) following the manufacturers protocol. A total of 500 ng of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using SuperScript™ IV First-Strand Synthesis System (18091050, Thermo Fisher) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentivirus production and infection

The lentivirus was generated as described previously.69 Briefly shRNA or cDNA expression plasmid were transfected into 293T cells obtained from ATCC, along with the packaging vectors psPAX2 (a gift from Didier Trono; Addgene plasmid # 12260) and VSV.G (a gift from Tannishtha Reya; Addgene plasmid # 14888) using plain DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1:3 ratio of cDNA plasmid to polyethyleneimine (25 μg/μl) (9002-98-6, Fisher Scientific). Virus-containing media was collected 48 hrs after transfection followed by ultracentrifugation (Beckmann Coulter # XL-90) at 24000 rpm at 4°C for two hours. The virus pellet was resuspended in 250 μl of respective media for the cell lines which were to be infected. For genetic knock-down or overexpression experiments, 0.2 million cells were seeded onto 6 well plates in respective growth media without any antibiotics, the next day infection was done by adding concentrated virus without using polybrene. After 24 hours of infection, the cells were seeded onto 10 cm plates and successfully transduced cells were selected by using appropriate antibiotics. Puromycin (P7255, Sigma-Aldrich) was used at 1.25 μg/ml for cell lines LP6, T449, LPS853, LPS141, and 2.25 ug/ml for MEFs. Neomycin (10131027, Gibco) was used at 220 μg/ml for cell lines LP6, T449, LPS853 and 750 μg/ml for MEFs. The puromycin and neomycin selection was done for two days and 6 days respectively.

These were delivered in a lentiviral construct that also encoded neomycin resistance. After selection in neomycin (220 μg/ml) for 6 days, cells were counted and 1.0 million cells were seeded the day before the second infection with a control shRNA (shLuc) or shMETTL1 #1, both of which had a puromycin selection cassette. After 48 hours of puromycin selection (1.25 μg/ml), cell counting was performed to collect 0.5 million cells for western blot and to seed 0.2 million cells onto 6 well plate in growth media without antibiotics. Cells were then counted using trypan blue after trypsinization every third day to get the absolute cell numbers followed by seeding equal number of cells. The total number of cells was calculated by multiplying absolute number of cells with the split factor that is the total number of cells divided by the number of cells seeded. The relative viability was calculated by dividing the total number of cells for each data point by the number of cells initially seeded on day 0 and plotted as XY format in GraphPad with the number of days at X axis and the relative viability on Y axis.

RNA extraction and tRNA charging assay

The RNA extraction and tRNA charging assay were performed as described.52 Briefly, it involved total RNA extraction from liposarcoma cells using TRIzol (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific) method using buffers with pH=4.5 to prevent de-aminoacylation of tRNA. Ribosomal RNA quantification

2 million LPS853 and LPS141 cells at 70-80 % confluence were incubated with 1 μCi/mL 5,6-3 H-uridine (NET367250UC, Revvity) for 1 hour at 37°C in a cell culture incubator. Ribosome isolation was performed as described.71 Briefly, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS followed by the use of a cell lifter to collect cells in a 50 ml tube. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 minutes, followed by carefully discarding the supernatant and gentle dissociation in a 3:1 ratio of cellular pellet to buffer A (250 mM sucrose, 250 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4). The resuspended pellet was incubated on ice for 10-15 minutes after adding 0.7% (v/v) NP-40, and homogenized through repeated pipetting. The lysate was centrifuged for 10 minutes at 750 g to pellet nuclei. The supernatant containing the cytoplasmic fraction was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged for 10 minutes at 12,500 g to separate the mitochondrial pellet from the supernatant containing the ribosomes. The ribosomal fraction was transferred to a new tube and the volume was carefully noted in order to add KCl (from a 4 M stock solution) to a final concentration of 0.5 M. One ml of the sucrose cushion was added to the SETON tubes, and the KCl supplemented ribosomal fraction was then introduced above it. Balancing was done using buffer B (250 mM sucrose, 500 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4), followed by ultracentrifugation at 250,000 g for two hours. The supernatant was discarded, the translucent pellet with the ribosomes was quickly rinsed twice using 100 μl of cold water, and then carefully removed by aspiration without disturbing the pellet. The ribosomal pellet was resuspended in 150 μl of buffer C (25 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.4). RNA was purified using an RNeasy kit (74106, Qiagen), and quantified using nanodrop. Uridine incorporation was assessed in equal amounts of rRNA extracted from LPS853 and LPS141 cells, using 10 mL of Ultima Gold scintillation buffer (Perkin-Elmer), and scintillation counts were measured using a Tri-Carb 2910 TR instrument (Perkin-Elmer).

Ribosome profiling and RNA sequencing

Ribosome profiling was performed as detailed previously.72 In brief, 10 million cells per replicate were grown to 60-70% confluence. Cells were then harvested, washed once in 1x cold PBS, and collected by centrifugation. Cells were lysed in a buffer of 20 mM Tris HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiotrietol, 0.05% NP-40 and 25 U/mL Turbo-DNase I (Invitrogen). 2 μg/mL of cyclohexamide was additionally added to the lysis buffer. Cleared lysates were quantified, and an aliquot was set aside for RNA sequencing. To the ribosome profiling sample, 2.5 U/μg of RNAse I was added for 45 minutes at room temperature for digestion. The reaction was quenched with an equal volume of 1 U/μL Superase RNase Inhibitor (Ambion). Ribosome protected fragments (RPFs) were isolated using ultracentrifugation at 55,000 RPM at 4°C for 2 hours. RPF RNA was purified with a Zymo Direct-Zol kit (Zymo) and rRNA was depleted using the siTOOLS human riboPOOL kit (siTOOLS Biotech, Germany). Denatured ribosome protected fragments (RPFs) were resolved on a 15% TBE-Urea gel (200V, 65 minutes) and the RPF band at 26-32 nt was excised and RNA extracted. After RNA precipitation, RNA was end-repaired with T4 PNK at 37°C for 1 hour, cleaned up with RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo). The 3' linker adapter was ligated (22°C, 3hrs) using T4 RNA ligase I and R4 RNA ligase 2 deletion mutant. Linker reactions were removed with Rec J exonuclease (Lucigen) and 5’ deadenylase (NEB). cDNA was synthesized with EpiScript RT enzyme (Lucigen, 50C for 30 minutes). After clean-up with Exonuclease I and RNAse I and hybridase (Lucigen), cDNA was resolved on a 10% TBE-Urea gel (175 V for 1 hour), and 75 nt bands were excised and DNA was precipitated. Precipitated cDNA was circularized with CircLigase (Lucigen, 60oC for 3 hr). Circular cDNA was amplified using 2x Phusion HiFi master mix (NEB) in a 11-14 cycle PCR reaction depending on cDNA yield. PCR products were precipitated with ethanol and 5 M NaCl, and resolved on a 8% TBE gel (100 V, 90 minutes). The 150 nt band was excised, purified, quantified for DNA abundance using a DNA Qubit, and analyzed for library size using an Agilent TapeStation (Agilent). Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq.

For RNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted from an aliquot of the lysate used for ribosome profiling using a Zymo Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo) and quantified using the Qubit RNA HS Assay (Invitrogen). Poly-A enrichment and library preparation was carried out in the Advanced Genomics Core at the University of Michigan. Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq.

For processing and mapping of ribosome profiling and RNA sequencing data, Ensembl assembly GRCm39 version 112 was used as the reference transcriptome. Trimming was performed using cutadapt version 4.4. STAR version 2.7.11a was used to filter contaminant RNA and DNA from ribosome profiling data using the additional arguments “SeedSearchStartLmaxOverLread .5”, “outFilterMultimapNmax 1000”, “outFilterMismatchNmax 2”, and “outReadsUnmapped Fastx”. Mapping was performed using STAR, using the flags “outFilterMultimapNmax 10”, “outMultimapperOrder Random”, “outFilterMismatchNmax 2”, “seedSearchStartLmaxOverLread 0.5”, and “alignEndsType EndToEnd”. RNA-seq reads were mapped using the additional arguments “–outMultimapperOrder Random”. Quantification of the sequencing reads was performed using Salmon v1.10.1 using the mapped read files.

Equal amounts of peptide from biological replicates of each condition (4 conditions x 3 replicates = 12 samples) were combined and fractionated by offline HPLC (0-35%B in 60 minutes, A=20 mM ammonium formate, B=acetonitrile; column=10 cm x 150 μm XBridge C18, 5 μm, 120Å , Waters, Milford, MA) directly to EVO tips (~300 ng peptide per tip; Odense, Denmark) using a micro fraction collector.73 Peptides were analyzed with a 20-SPD method on an EVO Sep One (column=75 μm I.D. packed with 15 cm 1.5 μm Dr. Maisch C18 [ESI Source Solutions, Woburn, MA] with integrated emitter tip74 coupled to a Bruker timsTOF Flex mass spectrometer operated in DDA-PASEF mode. Spectra were searched against a forward-reversed mouse reference proteome (Swiss Prot) containing tomato, EGFP, AKT2, and CMETTL1 sequences, as well as laboratory contaminants. QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

For two-group comparisons, a Student’s t-test was used to assess significance. For 3-group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, and a Dunnett adjustment for multiple comparisons was used. For analysis of two effects, a two-way ANOVA was performed with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. The log-rank test was used to test for differences in tumor onset between groups of zebrafish. Data shown as bar graphs without individual data points represent the mean +/- standard error of the mean (SEM) of a minimum of 3 biologic replicates. Experiments that included two biologic replicates per condition are shown as bar graphs that also include individual symbols for each data point. All p values reported are two-sided and considered significant if <0.05. All cell culture experiments were repeated independently at least twice to ensure reproducibility.

Differential expression analysis of RNA-seq and Ribo-seq data was performed using edgeR,78 following a model design integrating both a factor for sequencing type (Ribo-/RNA-seq) and treatment group. An orthogonal design matrix was formulated to facilitate the comparison across correlated factors. After filtering lowly expressed genes, normalization factors were determined using the TMM (trimmed mean of M-values) method. Dispersion estimates were calculated for the dataset, setting a threshold with a minimum total count of mapped reads of 30 across the samples. Subsequent to model fitting, specific contrasts were established and evaluated for both aggregated and independent comparisons across sequencing conditions such as Ribo and RNA levels, as well as comparisons involving translational efficiency differences among various treatment groups.
