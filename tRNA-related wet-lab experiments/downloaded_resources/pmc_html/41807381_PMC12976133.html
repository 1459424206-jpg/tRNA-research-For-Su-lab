Abstract

Modifications in the anticodon region of transfer RNA (tRNA) are essential for accurate and efficient protein synthesis. 5-Methyl-2-thiouridine derivatives (xm5s2U) are major modifications at the wobble position of tRNA anticodons decoding purine-ending two-codon sets. Although the thiocarbonyl group of xm5s2U enhances decoding efficiency, it is chemically susceptible to oxidative desulfuration, yielding 4-pyrimidinone derivatives (xm5h2U). Here, we identify xm5h2U derivatives in human cells and mouse tissues and confirm their cellular formation by spike-in experiments. Desulfurized tRNAs carrying 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl-4-pyrimidinone (mcm5h2U) show impaired codon recognition in a human reconstituted in vitro translation system. The mcm5h2U modification reduces aminoacylation of tRNAs for lysine, glutamate, and glutamine, but not arginine. Cryogenic electron microscopy reveals the structural basis of altered AAA/AAG decoding by mcm5h2U at the ribosomal A-site. These findings reveal a mechanism by which oxidative desulfuration of tRNA modifications dynamically regulates codon recognition and protein synthesis under oxidative stress conditions in human and mammalian cells.

Subject terms: RNA modification, tRNAs, Cryoelectron microscopy

Wobble‑position tRNA modifications support accurate decoding, but how cells regulate them is unclear. Here, the authors identify xm5h2U, an oxidized form of xm5s2U, in mammals and show these impair aminoacylation and codon recognition, revealing a stress‑responsive mechanism controlling translation.

Introduction

Higher-order biological processes emerge from sophisticated regulation of gene expression at multiple stages at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, including translational control. In protein synthesis, transfer RNA (tRNA) anticodons recognize and decode messenger RNA (mRNA) codons on the ribosome. tRNAs undergo the most extensive and diverse range of post-transcriptional modifications, particularly within the anticodon region. To date, ~150 types of RNA modifications have been identified in various RNA molecules across all domains of life, with tRNAs comprising over 80% of these modifications1. Recent research indicates that tRNA modifications respond dynamically to cellular metabolite levels and environmental stresses, temporally and spatially influencing translational regulation significantly2–7.

The anticodon of tRNA, located at positions 34, 35, and 36, recognizes a specific codon on the mRNA through base pairing at the A-site of the ribosome. In this interaction, the first and second nucleotides of the codon form Watson–Crick base pairs with the third (position 36) and second (position 35) nucleotides of the anticodon, respectively. These two Watson–Crick base pairs in the codon–anticodon helix are strictly monitored through A-minor interactions8 with conserved residues in the decoding center of the small ribosomal subunit (corresponding to A1492, A1493, and G530 of the bacterial 16S rRNA)9. These interactions trigger a large conformational rearrangement of the small subunit and induce GTP hydrolysis by the elongation factor bound to the ribosome, thereby ensuring accurate decoding during protein synthesis. By contrast, base pairing between the third nucleotide of the codon and the first nucleotide (position 34) of the anticodon does not necessarily follow the Watson–Crick pairing rules; instead, wobble base pairing is permitted10. This mechanism allows 61 sense codons to be efficiently decoded by a limited number of tRNAs. The first position of the anticodon often contains diverse modifications that promote or modulate codon–anticodon interactions, thereby enabling accurate and efficient decoding of the genetic code11–13. Defects in tRNA modifications frequently impair multiple stages of protein synthesis—including aminoacylation, decoding, and translocation—and can lead to pathological consequences2,14–16.

2-Thiouridine (s2U) and its derivatives (xm5s2U; Fig. 1a, b) are highly conserved wobble modifications found at the first position of the anticodon in all domains of life17–19. In bacteria and archaea, 5-methylaminomethyl-2-thiouridine (mnm5s2U) and 5-carboxymethylaminomethyl-2-thiouridine (cmnm5s2U) occur in tRNAs for Glu, Gln, and Lys. In eukaryotes, 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl-2-thiouridine (5-methylcarboxymethyl-2-thiouridine, mcm5s2U)20 and 5-taurinomethyl-2-thiouridine (τm5s2U)21 are present in cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs for Lys, Glu and Gln, respectively22,23. Mammalian cytosolic tRNAArg responsible for AGR codons also has mcm5s2U24. Recent studies identified 2′-O-methyl-2-thiouridine (s2Um) and 5-cyanomethyl-2-thiouridine (cnm5s2U) in archaeal tRNAs25–27. The s2U derivatives are also present at position 54 of tRNAs. 5-Methyl-2-thiouridine (m5s2U) and 1-methyl-4-thiopseudouridine (m1s4Ψ) are present in thermophilic bacteria and archaea28–30.

The xm5s2U modification performs pleiotropic roles in translation, including efficient aminoacylation of tRNAs31–33, accurate and efficient decoding of purine-ending codons (NNR)17,34, and rapid translocation on the ribosome35. The xm5s2U modification favors a C3′-endo sugar pucker conformation due to steric repulsion between 2-thiocarbonyl and 2′-hydroxyl groups36,37, so that xm5s2U prevents misdecoding of pyrimidine-ending codons (NNY). In addition, structural analyses revealed that mnm5s2U34 base pairs with the third guanosine of NNG codons at the A-site of the ribosome via an anionic U • G wobble pairing38,39. In this base pairing geometry, the uracil base of xm5s2U34 moves toward the minor groove39, rather than the major groove as occurs in canonical U • G wobble pairing10. In this geometry, 2-thio group of xm5s2U contributes to stacking interaction with the second base pair of the codon–anticodon helix38. To make this wobble pair possible, N3 of the uracil base should be deprotonated. In fact, s2U modification promotes acidity of the N3 atom of the uracil base by lowering its pKa value40. This is the molecular basis by which xm5s2U facilitates the decoding of G-ending codons. However, according to structural study of the anticodon-stem loop bound to 30S ribosomal subunit41, the mcm5s2U–G base pair adopts a Watson–Crick-like geometry, and it was proposed that mcm5s2U may undergo a tautomeric shift of the uracil base from keto-to-enol form, allowing it to form non-canonical hydrogen bonds with G.

Biogenesis of 2-thio modification of xm5s2U34 has been extensively studied in both bacteria and eukaryotes17,18,30. Cysteine desulfurase (bacterial IscS and eukaryotic Nfs1) initiates s2U formation by extracting a sulfur atom from cysteine as a persulfide, which is then relayed by several sulfur mediators and transferred to U34 of tRNA by thiouridylases (bacterial MnmA and the eukaryotic CTU1/CTU2 complex)42–46. In bacteria, the sulfur is relayed by multiple sulfur mediators through persulfide chemistry45, whereas in eukaryotes, sulfur transfer is facilitated by a ubiquitin-activating E1-like enzyme and ubiquitin-like proteins43,47. In bacteria, loss of s2U modification due to deficiency of mnmA or sulfur mediators causes a slow growth phenotype46, highlighting the physiological importance of this modification. Disruption of the CTU1/CTU2 complex impairs cytoplasmic s2U modification in yeast48, worms42, and plants49. Intriguingly, loss of cytoplasmic s2U showed a synthetic lethality together with deficiency of 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl modification of mcm5s2U48. On the other hand, loss of mitochondrial s2U modification due to mutation of the yeast mitochondrial tRNA-specific 2-thiouridylase gene mtu1 resulted in deficiency of mitochondrial protein synthesis and respiratory function50. It is known that pathogenic mutations in the human MTU1 gene cause reversible infantile liver failure (RILF), a heritable and acute liver failure followed by spontaneous recovery at an early stage of life51,52. Loss-of-function mutations in the CTU2 gene cause severe primary microcephaly, ambiguous male genitalia, dysmorphic facies, polydactyly, and renal agenesis (DREAM-PL)53,54.

Due to the chemical properties of the thiocarbonyl group, xm5s2U modifications are chemically unstable, and readily oxidized and desulfurized under in vitro oxidative conditions, forming uridine or 4-pyrimidinone derivatives (xm5h2U) (Fig. 1b)55,56. h2U predominantly adopts the C2′-endo form57, suggesting less stable base pairing with A or G than s2U. Synthetic RNA duplexes containing an h2U • A pair exhibited significantly lower melting temperatures than those with an s2U • A pair, while duplexes with either an s2U • G or h2U • G pair displayed similar melting temperatures58. If desulfuration of xm5s2U occurs in cells, xm5h2U34 is likely to negatively regulate protein synthesis. In fact, mcm5h2U and cm5h2U, the desulfurized forms of mcm5s2U and cm5s2U, have been found in tRNAs from yeast and various human cultured cells regardless of the presence or absence of oxidative stress, reaching up to 11% depending on the efficiency of the antioxidant defense mechanisms59. However, their mechanistic roles in translational regulation have not been elucidated.

Through a spike-in experiment, we confirmed that h2U derivatives are naturally occurring in cellular processes, rather than artifacts produced during tRNA extraction and purification. The mcm5h2U34 modification significantly impairs the aminoacylation efficiency of tRNAs for Lys, Glu, and Gln, eliminates AAA decoding, and substantially decreases AAG decoding at the A-site of the ribosome. This alteration affects protein synthesis in a human reconstituted in vitro translation system. Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structural analyses of ribosomes complexed with desulfurized tRNAs and AAR codons showed that the mcm5h2U • G pair has a minor groove-shifted U • G wobble geometry, whereas the mcm5h2U • A pair adopts a unique geometry with a single hydrogen bond (H-bond). These findings indicate that oxidative desulfuration of tRNA modifications influences protein synthesis under oxidative stress conditions.

Results

Detection of h2U derivatives in mammalian and bacterial tRNAs

We reviewed our previous liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) data on cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs in mammalian tissues and human culture cells22, and detected unassigned nucleosides with a molecular mass 32 Da lower than that of mcm5s2U (m/z 333) and τm5s2U (m/z 398), implying that h2U derivatives are indeed present in cellular tRNAs. In LC-MS nucleoside analyses of total RNA from mouse livers, we clearly detected unassigned nucleosides with m/z 301 (Fig. 1c). In addition, we also detected nucleosides with m/z 366 in mitochondrial tRNALys isolated from pig liver (Fig. 1d). Collision-induced dissociation (CID) of these nucleosides generated base-related ions (BH2+) with loss of unmodified ribose (Fig. 1e, f), suggesting that the 32 Da loss originates from their bases.

To confirm that these nucleosides are h2U derivatives, we prepared authentic h2U derivatives by in vitro oxidation of mouse tRNA fraction and pig mitochondrial tRNALys, and found massive accumulation of mcm5h2U (Fig. 1c) and τm5h2U (Fig. 1d). Next, we conducted LC-MS co-injection analyses of nucleosides from total RNA and its in vitro oxidized form, and demonstrated that the unassigned nucleosides co-eluted with the authentic h2U derivatives (Fig. 1c, d), confirming the presence of mcm5h2U and τm5h2U in mammalian tissues.

We also detected an unassigned nucleoside (m/z 272) with molecular mass 32 Da lower than that of mnm5s2U (m/z 304) in Escherichia coli tRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). LC-MS co-injection with in vitro oxidized total tRNA confirmed the identity of this nucleoside as mnm5h2U (Supplementary Fig. 1a). To test whether mnm5h2U arises from desulfuration of mnm5s2U, we analyzed nucleosides from the E. coli ΔmnmA strain, which lacks 2-thiolation. In this strain, neither mnm5s2U nor mnm5h2U was detected (Supplementary Fig. 1c), supporting that mnm5h2U is derived from mnm5s2U. Similarly, we analyzed cytoplasmic tRNAGln from wild-type Saccharomyces cerevisiae and a ctu1Δ strain lacking 2-thiolation of mcm5s2U (Supplementary Fig. 1d). In wild-type tRNAGln, mcm5s2U was detected as the major modification, whereas the ctu1Δ strain showed only mcm5U and no detectable mcm5h2U (Supplementary Fig. 1d). These results confirm that mcm5h2U, like mnm5h2U, is generated from the corresponding 2-thiouridine derivative.

Confirmation of h2U derivatives produced in cells

Given the chemical nature of xm5s2U modifications, we initially hypothesized that their h2U derivatives might result from artificial oxidation during preparation of tRNAs in vitro. To confirm that h2U derivatives, namely,  mcm5h2U, τm5h2U, and h2U, are indeed generated in mammalian cells, we conducted a spike-in experiment using E. coli tRNA as a tracer. This approach allowed us to estimate the extent of artificial oxidation of xm5s2U during in vitro handling of tRNAs. As a tracer, we isolated E. coli tRNAGln1, which bears cmnm5s2U at position 34 of the anticodon (Fig. 2a). This tRNA was chosen because cmnm5s2U and its desulfurized derivative, cmnm5h2U, are distinct from xm5s2U and xm5h2U found in mammalian tRNAs. In our experiment, E. coli tRNAGln1 was added to a mouse liver homogenate as a spike-in tracer, followed by extraction of total RNA and purification of individual tRNAs, including the tracer, using reciprocal circulating chromatography (RCC)60 (Fig. 2a).

To monitor artificial desulfuration of s2U derivatives during RNA extraction and tRNA purification, we measured the rate of conversion of cmnm5s2U to cmnm5h2U in the tracer tRNA (Fig. 2a). We isolated cytoplasmic tRNALys and tRNAGln, as well as mitochondrial tRNALys and tRNAGlu, along with E. coli tRNAGln1 as a tracer from mouse liver lysates. The tracer tRNA was then digested with RNase T1, and its anticodon-containing fragments were analyzed by capillary LC nano-electrospray ionization (ESI)-MS, revealing the presence of cmnm5s2U, cmnm5h2U, and cmnm5U (Fig. 2b). We detected cmnm5h2U in ~2% of the tracer tRNA prior to the spike-in experiment (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2a). CID analysis confirmed the presence of cmnm5h2U at position 34 (Fig. 2c). After isolation with mouse tRNAs, the level of cmnm5h2U remained virtually unchanged (Fig. 2b, d, and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c), indicating that artificial formation of h2U derivatives during handling of tRNAs was negligible under our experimental conditions.

Using this approach, we identified mcm5h2U in 9.9% and 7.2% of mouse cytoplasmic tRNALys and tRNAGln, respectively (Fig. 3a–d). CID analyses confirmed the presence of mcm5h2U at position 34 in both tRNAs (Fig. 3e, f). Similarly, τm5h2U was detected in 2.3% and 2.9% of mitochondrial tRNALys and tRNAGlu, respectively (Fig. 3g–j). h2U was also identified in 0.4% and 1.3% of these tRNAs, respectively (Fig. 3g–j). The presence of τm5h2U at position 34 in both mitochondrial tRNAs was further validated by CID analyses (Fig. 3k, l).

To expand on these findings, we performed similar analyses in various cultured cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 3). We observed that mcm5h2U was present at varying levels across different cell lines. Notably, ~15% of tRNALys in HeLa cells contained mcm5h2U, while it was scarcely detected in A549 and T24 cells.

Translation efficiency decreases upon desulfuration of tRNAs

Given that 2-thiouridine derivatives play crucial roles in accurate and efficient translation, we wondered whether h2U derivatives affect translation in a codon-specific manner. We examined the function of desulfurized tRNAs using a human cell-free translation system. To this end, we prepared desulfurized tRNAs by in vitro oxidation with H2O256 and confirmed efficient conversion of mcm5s2U to mcm5h2U (Supplementary Fig. 4a). As a reporter mRNA, Renilla luciferase (Rluc) with an N-terminal hemagglutinin (HA) was used (Fig. 4a), and translation initiation was mediated by the Hepatitis C virus (HCV) internal ribosome entry site (IRES) (Fig. 4a). As a control mRNA, we employed a mutant HiBiT peptide that is unaffected by tRNA desulfuration (Fig. 4a). Since mcm5s2U is present in four mammalian cytoplasmic tRNAs for Lys, Glu, Gln, and Arg (encoded by AGR codons), two Lys residues in the HiBiT peptide (VSGWRLFKKIS) were substituted with Arg encoded by CGN codons to generate a mutant HiBiT construct (SGWRLFRRIS) which forms a complex with Large BiT, reconstituting functional nano luciferase activity.

The Rluc reporter mRNA and either desulfurized or untreated total tRNA were added to a reconstituted human cell-free protein synthesis system consisting of human 80S ribosomes, translation factors, and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases61. The activity of Rluc was measured by luciferase assay. As expected, significant Rluc activity was observed when untreated tRNA was used, whereas it was markedly reduced when desulfurized tRNA was used (Fig. 4b). This result was corroborated by western blotting using an anti-HA antibody (Fig. 4c). To demonstrate that this effect is specific to certain codons, the mutant HiBiT mRNA was translated and nano luciferase activity was measured. We observed similar levels of activity regardless of whether desulfurized or untreated tRNA was used (Fig. 4b). Among 378 amino acid residues in the Rluc reporter sequence, 77 codons are read by the four tRNA species containing mcm5s2U, accounting for 22% of the total (Supplementary Data 1). Specifically, it consists of 62 A-ending codons and 15 G-ending codons. The G-ending codons are redundantly decoded by isodecoders having either mcm5s2U34 or C34, whereas the A-ending codons are decoded solely by mcm5s2U34-tRNAs. These findings suggest that the translational activity of mcm5s2U-modified tRNAs is impaired by desulfuration.

Since desulfurized tRNAs had less activity in an in vitro translation, we next examined whether desulfurized tRNAs participate in protein synthesis in cells by detecting mcm5h2U in polysome-associated tRNAs. HEK293T cell lysate was first separated into the supernatant (free RNA) and crude ribosome fractions using a sucrose cushion (Fig. 4d), followed by sucrose density gradient (SDG) centrifugation to obtain the polysome fraction (Fig. 4e). Small RNA were then obtained from the free RNA and polysomal RNA, and subjected to the LC-MS nucleoside analysis, showing that mcm5h2U is present in the polysomal RNA (Fig. 4f, g). As expected, the signal intensity of mcm5h2U in the polysomal RNA was lower than that in the total RNA and free RNA fractions. These results suggest that a portion of desulfurized tRNAs participates in translation, whereas others might be excluded.

Effect of tRNA desulfuration on aminoacylation

It has been revealed that the desulfuration of mcm5s2U impairs protein synthesis. We next investigated which step of protein synthesis is affected by mcm5h2U. Although 2-thio modification of xm5s2U is known to contribute to efficient aminoacylation of tRNAs in bacteria31–33, the role of the 2-thio group of mcm5s2U in tRNA aminoacylation remains poorly understood in mammals. To examine the effect of desulfuration of mcm5s2U on aminoacylation, we performed in vitro aminoacylation reactions using recombinant human aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and total tRNAs with or without desulfuration.

As a control, we used tRNAsThr, which do not have mcm5s2U. Little change in Thr incorporation in total tRNAs was observed regardless of desulfuration treatment (Fig. 5a). Next, we compared the incorporation activities of four amino acids (Arg, Gln, Glu, and Lys) into total tRNAs with or without desulfuration. For Arg, no difference in the incorporation efficiency was observed between desulfurized and untreated total tRNA (Fig. 5b), indicating that arginyl-tRNA synthetase (RARS1) does not recognize mcm5s2U34 in tRNAArg(AGR) for its aminoacylation. By contrast, significant reductions in aminoacylation activity were observed for tRNAs for Gln, Glu, and Lys upon desulfuration (Fig. 5c–e). Considering that tRNAs charged with these amino acids are a mixture of isoacceptors including isodecoders (Supplementary Data 2)62, some parts of which carry mcm5s2U34, it became evident that desulfuration of mcm5s2U34 significantly impairs aminoacylation of these tRNAs. Thus, we found that the three aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, glutaminyl-tRNA synthetase (QARS1), glutamyl-prolyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (EPRS1), and lysyl-tRNA synthetase (KARS1), specifically recognize the chemical structure of the 2-thiocarbonyl group in mcm5s2U to achieve efficient aminoacylation. These findings indicate that desulfuration of mcm5s2U negatively regulates cytoplasmic translation by lowering the aminoacylation efficiency of tRNAs for Gln, Glu, and Lys.

Effect of tRNA desulfuration on codon recognition

Next, we investigated the effect of mcm5s2U desulfuration on tRNA codon recognition on the ribosome. The xm5s2U34 modification is crucial for accurate and efficient recognition of purine nucleosides at the third position of NNR codons17,34. We measured the ability of human tRNALys with or without desulfuration to bind both AAA and AAG codons at the A-site on the ribosome. As a positive control, untreated tRNALys efficiently bound to both codons (Fig. 6a, b). Desulfurized tRNALys showed almost no binding to the AAA codon (Fig. 6a) and a ~60% reduction in binding to the AAG codon (Fig. 6b). These results reveal that mcm5h2U34 significantly impairs codon recognition at the A-site on the ribosome.

Structural insights into codon recognition mediated by h2U derivatives

To obtain structural insights into the molecular mechanism by which mcm5h2U34 reduces codon recognition ability, we conducted cryo-EM single-particle analyses of the ribosome complex with human cytoplasmic tRNALys bearing mcm5s2U34 or mcm5h2U34, P-site tRNA, and mRNA with AAA or AAG at the A-site. To this end, human cytoplasmic tRNALys was desulfurized by treating twice with H2O2, confirming the high frequency of mcm5h2U34 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). After several classification and focused classification targeting A-site tRNA, we determined cryo-EM structures of four ribosome complexes with A- and P-site tRNAs at 2.26–3.18 Å resolution (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Fig. 5a–d, Supplementary Fig. 6, and Supplementary Data 3). Ribosomes adopt the closed conformation in all four structures (Fig. 7b), with the codon-anticodon helix at the A-site strictly recognized by A-minor interactions with A1492 and A1493 in helix 44 and G530 in helix 18 of 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA). In particular, we clearly observed cryo-EM densities for 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl- and 2-thiocarbonyl groups of mcm5s2U34 (Fig. 7c). On the other hand, no density was observed for the 2-thiocarbonyl group in mcm5h2U34 (Fig. 7d). Additionally, the 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl groups of mcm5s2U34 and mcm5h2U34 interact with C1054 of 16S rRNA in all four structures (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d).

The mcm5s2U34 modification pairs with A3 of the AAA codon via a canonical Watson-Crick geometry (Fig. 7c), whereas mcm5s2U34 pairs with G3 of the AAG codon via a non-canonical base pairing involving a minor groove-shifted U (Fig. 7c). The geometry of the mcm5s2U • G3 base pair is discussed in more detail in the Supplementary Discussion. Meanwhile, mcm5h2U34 pairs with A3 via a unique geometry involving a single H-bond between O4 of mcm5h2U34 and N6 of A3 (Fig. 7d, e). The N3 atom of mcm5h2U no longer acts as a proton donor due to its chemical nature, so that it is not involved in any additional H-bonding (Fig. 7e). The observed geometry with a single H-bond could explain why s2U-to-h2U conversion results in loss of the ability to recognize the AAA codon (Fig. 6a).

The mcm5h2U • G pair adopts a similar geometry to the non-canonical wobble base pair mnm5s2U • G, with U* shifted into the minor groove (Fig. 7d, e). Given that the 2-thiocarbonyl group of mcm5s2U stabilizes C3′ endo ribose puckering36,37, mcm5s2U • G pairing should be more stable than mcm5h2U • G pairing. In addition, the 2-thiocarbonyl group of mcm5s2U lowers the pKa of N3 atom40, and the 5-methylcarboxymethyl group also contributes to the structural rearrangement to form the minor groove-shifted mcm5s2U • G pairing38. This structural observation is consistent with our biochemical evidence showing that s2U-to-h2U conversion decreases the ability to decode the AAG codon (Fig. 6b).

Discussion

Through a spike-in experiment, we demonstrated that 2-thiouridine derivatives in tRNA are desulfurized in cells and converted to h2U derivatives, but these are relatively scarce tRNA modifications; in mouse tissues, the frequency of mcm5h2U was <10% of cytoplasmic tRNAs bearing mcm5s2U (Fig. 3b, d), and τm5h2U was detected in only a few percent of mitochondrial tRNAs with τm5s2U (Fig. 3h, j). However, the abundance of h2U derivatives varies in different cell types. Approximately 15% of tRNALys in HeLa cells contained mcm5h2U, whereas little was detected in A549 and T24 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Since h2U derivatives can be generated by oxidative desulfuration in vitro55,56, it is highly likely that they are chemically produced in cells by reactive oxygen species generated under oxidative stress conditions. Nevertheless, enzymatic formation of h2U derivatives cannot be ruled out. In vitro oxidative desulfuration of s2U derivatives yields both h2U and uridine derivatives; however, since the desulfuration mechanism in cells remains unclear, the ratio in which these two types of products are formed is unknown. While h²U derivatives are likely to represent a form of damaged RNA, the uridine derivatives generated by desulfuration may be re-thiolated back to 2-thiouridine, suggesting that this is a reversible modification. Understanding the mechanisms underlying the differing frequencies of h2U derivatives in various cells and tissues is intriguing. The frequency of h²U is likely determined by multiple factors, including the cellular oxidative stress response, redox balance, and the degradation of h²U-modified tRNAs. Because s2U derivatives play a crucial role in codon-specific translation, our discovery suggests that tRNA s2U modification serves as a sensor to monitor intracellular oxidative states to regulate codon-specific translation (Fig. 8). In fact, although we quantified h2U derivatives under various oxidative stress conditions, we have not yet identified any conditions that reproducibly increase h2U levels (Supplementary Data 4). Sierant et al.59 likewise detected mcm5h2U in yeast and human cultured cells and analyzed its behavior under oxidative stress using calibration curves derived from synthetic standards; however, their results also showed that the levels of h2U species do not markedly increase upon exogenous oxidative stress. Cells counteract reactive oxygen species (ROS) primarily through enzymatic antioxidant systems, such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase63. At supraphysiological concentrations of H2O2, however, these enzymatic ROS-scavenging systems are rapidly overwhelmed, leading to cell death. Under these conditions, the limited impact of exogenous oxidative stress on h2U levels observed in our experiments (Supplementary Data 4) is more plausibly explained by the chemical buffering capacity of the intracellular environment, including abundant proteins, metabolites, and other redox-active components, rather than by regulated enzymatic antioxidant defenses64,65. The capacity and contribution of these mechanisms vary substantially among cell types, and this variation might be reflected in the different h2U levels observed across the cell lines examined (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Cell-free protein synthesis experiments using human translational apparatus reconstituted in vitro revealed that h2U derivatives suppress codon-specific translation (Fig. 4b, c). Specifically, h2U derivatives decreased the aminoacylation efficiency of cytoplasmic tRNAs for Lys, Gln, and Glu, but not for Arg (Fig. 5b–e). We also showed that τm5h2U and h2U are present in mitochondrial tRNAs (Fig. 3h, j), and it is plausible that they also suppress aminoacylation of mitochondrial tRNAs. Furthermore, in the codon recognition of cytoplasmic tRNALys, mcm5h2U almost abolished the ability to recognize the AAA codon and significantly reduced recognition of the AAG codon (Fig. 6a, b). Similar effects can be predicted for other cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs containing h2U derivatives, although the degree of suppression likely varies depending on the codons. Additionally, given that s2U modifications in bacterial tRNAs are involved in efficient translocation35, it is probable that h2U derivatives regulate translocation efficiency on mammalian ribosomes. Mass spectrometric analyses of the polysomal RNA clearly detected mcm5h2U (Fig. 4f, g), but its level was lower than that in the total RNA and free RNA fractions, indicating that a part of desulfurized tRNAs participates in translation, whereas the others do not due to their low abilities of aminoacylation and decoding.

Cryo-EM analysis of ribosome complexes revealed the structural basis for codon recognition by mcm5h2U34 at the A-site of the ribosome (Fig. 7d, e). While the base pairing geometry of the mcm5h2U34 • A3 pair resembles the Watson-Crick type, it involves only a single H-bond (Fig. 7e), indicating a very weak interaction. This result is consistent with previous findings on the thermodynamic stability of double-stranded RNAs containing h²U, as well as with their quantum chemical calculations58.

By contrast, the mcm5h2U34 • G3 pair adopts a geometry similar to the minor groove-shifted U • G wobble pair observed in the mcm5s2U34 • G3 pair, with the uracil base shifted toward the minor groove. This geometry arises because the N3 atom of mcm5h2U34 functions as a proton acceptor for the 2-amino group of G3 (Fig. 7e). However, unlike the mcm5s2U34 • G3 pair (Fig. 7c), the mcm5h2U34 • G3 pair lacks the stabilization provided by the 2-thiocarbonyl group (Fig. 7d, e), which enhances stacking effect with a neighboring base pair. As a result, the mcm5h2U34 • G3 pair is relatively weaker.

Building on these findings, we propose a hypothetical mechanism for translation regulation mediated by desulfurized tRNA modifications (Fig. 8). Under oxidative stress conditions, the mcm5s2U34 modification of cytoplasmic tRNAs for Lys, Gln, Glu, and Arg undergoes desulfuration, yielding mcm5h2U34 or mcm5U34. This leads to reduced aminoacylation efficiency for tRNALys, tRNAGln, and tRNAGlu, as well as impaired codon recognition at the ribosomal A-site. As a result, codon-specific translation efficiency is negatively impacted. Optimal translation speed is crucial for maintaining mRNA stability66–69 and ensuring correct folding of nascent proteins70. Therefore, desulfuration of xm5s2U modifications may cause codon-specific ribosome stalling, potentially triggering pathways such as codon optimality-mediated decay66 or the unfolded protein response71. Additionally, ribosome stalling during translation elongation could lead to ribosome collisions, activating the ribosome quality control pathway and inducing the integrated stress response or ribotoxic stress response72–74.

In this study, we demonstrated that tRNAs bearing h²U derivatives indeed participate in protein synthesis and that they markedly impair aminoacylation efficiency and codon recognition ability. Based on these findings, we performed a relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis (Supplementary Data 5) on genes induced under oxidative stress conditions (GO:0006979). Compared with the RSCU values calculated from all human protein-coding genes75, we observed no significant differences in codon usage for Lys, Gln, or Glu. However, for Arg, the usage of CGN codons was decreased, while that of AGR codons was relatively increased. Desulfuration of mcm5s2U led to reduced aminoacylation capacity in tRNAs for Lys, Gln, and Glu, whereas tRNAArg(ARG) retained full aminoacylation activity. Although the codon recognition properties of tRNAArg(AGR) bearing mcm⁵h²U remain to be investigated, its resistance to desulfuration may contribute to the preferential usage of AGR codons in oxidative stress-responsive genes.

In future work, our goal is to identify oxidative stress conditions that effectively promote the accumulation of h2U derivatives. This will allow us to further investigate the biological significance of desulfurized tRNA modifications in specific biological contexts.

Cell culture

E. coli cells were cultivated in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium with vigorous shaking at 37 °C. For oxidative stress conditions, HeLa cells were cultured until they reached 70–80% confluency, then treated for 1 h with oxidative stress-inducing agents: 2 mM H2O2 (Nacalai Tesque), 20 µM menadione (Sigma-Aldrich), or 0.25–1 mM rotenone (Tokyo Chemical Industry). Although 2 mM H2O2 is far above the IC50 value for HeLa cells (50 μM), we did not observe any cell detachment nor apoptosis in our condition. Following treatment, cells were washed with PBS. Total RNA was extracted as shown below.

RNA extraction and isolation of individual tRNAs

Total RNA was extracted from E. coli as described77. Briefly, E. coli cells were suspended in a 1:1 mixture of water-saturated phenol and RNA extraction buffer A [50 mM NaOAc (pH 5.2) and 10 mM Mg(OAc)2], followed by two cycles of freeze-thawing with liquid nitrogen, and vigorous stirring for 1 h at room temperature. After centrifugation, the aqueous phase was subjected to chloroform extraction and 2-propanol precipitation.

Total RNA from yeast cells were prepared as follows. Yeast cells were resuspended in 5–10 weights of RNA extraction buffer B [50 mM NaOAc (pH 5.2), 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, and 14.3 mM β-mercaptoethanol], and an equal volume of water-saturated phenol was added. The mixture was shaken for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, treated with an equal volume of chloroform, followed by isopropanol precipitation. The pellets were dissolved in water, and added with 2% (v/v) 1 M MES-NaOH (pH 6.0), 2.5% (v/v) 5 M NaCl, and 40% (v/v) 2-butoxyethanol. The mixture was then placed on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove precipitates. The supernatants were subjected to ethanol precipitation to obtain the total RNA. To obtain small RNA fraction, total RNA  was subjected to an anion-exchange resin (DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow; Cytiva) in low salt buffer A [20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)]. After washing the resin with the same buffer, small RNA fractions were eluted with high salt buffer A [20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl, and 2 mM DTT].

Total RNA from cultured cells or frozen tissue were prepared using an isolation reagent comprising 40% (w/v) phenol, 1% (v/v) ethylene glycol, 2 M guanidine thiocyanate, 20 mM tri-sodium citrate, 0.1 M NaOAc (pH 4.5), 0.2% (w/v) N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt, 50 mM salicylic acid, 10 µM aluminon, 6.9 mM 8-hydroxyquinoline, 0.0001 w/v% BPB. For highly reducing conditions, the same isolation reagent was used with the addition of 50 mM DTT. Alternatively, total RNA were extracted from cultured cells or frozen tissue using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Roche). Frozen tissues were cryogenically ground in a GRINDOMIX GM 200 knife mill (Roche) prior to extraction. For further purification, total RNA were subjected to an anion-exchange resin (DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow; Cytiva) in low salt buffer B [10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT], and small RNA fractions were eluted with high salt buffer B [10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl and 2 mM DTT]. To obtain total tRNA fractions or class I tRNA fractions, total RNA was separated by on 7 M urea denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and stained with SYBR Gold (Invitrogen), then purified by excising the corresponding gel bands. Individual tRNAs were isolated by RCC60,78. Sequences of 5′-terminal ethylcarbamate (EC) amino-modified DNA probes (Sigma Aldrich) complementary to target tRNAs are listed in Supplementary Data 6. Each probe was covalently immobilized on NHS-activated Sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin (Cytiva), packed in a custom-made tip, and attached to a custom-made multichannel head on the RCC device. Small RNA fractions obtained by DEAE chromatography were dissolved in 6 × NHE buffer [1.2 M NaCl, 30 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 15 mM EDTA-NaOH pH 8.0 and 1 mM DTT], and passed through the tip by auto-pipetting at 65 °C. After washing the tip columns with 0.1 × NHE at 40 °C, bound tRNAs were eluted with 0.1 × NHE at 68 °C. The purity of isolated tRNAs was confirmed by 10% PAGE with 7 M urea.

RNA mass spectrometry

Isolated tRNAs were digested with RNase T1 and analyzed by capillary LC/nano-ESI-MS as described78–80. One picomole of isolated tRNA was digested with 20 U of RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 20 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.3) at 37 °C for 45 min. A linear ion trap-orbitrap hybrid MS instrument (LTQ Orbitrap XL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was equipped with a splitless nano HPLC system (DiNa; Techno Alpha) using a nano-LC trap column (L-column2 ODS; 0.3 × 5 mm; CERI) and a capillary column (HiQ Sil C18W-3; 0.1 × 100 mm; Techno Alpha). Digested fragments were separated for 35 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with a linear gradient from 2 to 100% solvent B. The solvent system consisted of 0.4 M 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP; pH 7.0, solvent A) and 0.4 M HFIP (pH 7.0) in 50% methanol (solvent B). The eluent was ionized using an ESI source in negative mode, with a scan range of m/z 600–2000. Xcalibur 2.0.7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for system operation.

LC-MS shotgun analyses of tRNA modification were conducted as described81 with slight modification. Five picomoles of class I tRNA fraction was digested with 50 units of RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 20 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.3) at 37 °C for 45 min. Digested fragments were analyzed by a DiNa nano-flow HPLC system (Techno Alpha) coupled with an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The percentage intensity of each modification was calculated from the ratio of mass chromatogram peak areas of RNA fragments with and without the target modification using Qual Browser in Xcalibur 4.2.28.14 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Nucleoside analyses of tRNAs were performed as described79,82. Four micrograms of total RNA were digested into nucleosides with 0.1 U of nuclease P1 (Wako Pure Chemical Industries) in 10 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.3) for 1 h at 37 °C, followed sequentially by 0.1 U of phosphodiesterase I (PDase I; Worthington Biochemical Corporation) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and 0.16 U of bacterial alkaline phosphatase (BAP; E. coli C75; Takara). For hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC)/ESI-MS, digests were dissolved in 90% acetonitrile/10% water and applied to a ZIC-cHILIC column (3 μm particle size, 2.1 × 150 mm; Merck Millipore) connected to a Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Ultimate 3000 LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nucleosides were separated at a flow rate of 100 μL/min in a multistep gradient comprising 90-85% solvent B from 0 to 10 min, 85–30% solvent B from 10 to 30 min with gradient curve 7, 30% solvent B for 10 min, then a return to 90% solvent B. Proton adducts of nucleosides were scanned in positive ion mode across an m/z range of 110–950. Xcalibur 4.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for system operation.

Preparation of h2U-modified tRNAs by in vitro desulfuration

In vitro oxidation/desulfuration of s2U-modified tRNAs was conducted according to the literature56 with slight modification. Human or mouse tRNA mixtures (400 μg) were dissolved in 10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4) and 100 mM H2O2 and incubated for 24 h at room temperature, followed by performing ethanol precipitation twice. For individual tRNAs, the isolated tRNALys (40 μg) was dissolved in 10 mM HEPES-KOH buffer (pH 7.4) and 50 mM H2O2 and incubated for 24 h at room temperature, followed by ethanol precipitation twice. For complete desulfuration, the reaction was conducted twice to achieve the highest s2U-to-h2U conversion rate.

Preparation of mRNAs for cell-free translation

The pUC-T7-HCV-HA-Rluc plasmid was used for Rluc mRNA transcription. For the control, we designed pUC-T7-HCV-mHiBiT plasmid encoding a mutant HiBiT peptide with 27 amino acids (MSTNPNTLSTTGGSSGWRLFRRISGGS) that does not contain any Lys, Glu, Gln, or Arg residues (encoded by AGR codons). For plasmid construction, a DNA segment spanning T7 promotor, HCV-IRES, and mutant HiBiT sequences was PCR-amplified from the pUC-T7-HCV-HA-Rluc plasmid as a template, followed by a second round of PCR to add an XbaI restriction enzyme site to the 3′ end of the product. The amplified product was inserted into the HindIII and XbaI sites of the same vector. Finally, according to the literature83,84, the beginning of the HCV-IRES sequence was mutated from MSTNPKPQRKT to MSTNPNTLSTT to generate the pUC-T7-HCV-mHiBiT plasmid by site-directed mutagenesis to avoid codons read by h2U-containing tRNAs. All primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Plasmids were linearized by XbaI (Takara) digestion, and mRNAs were synthesized by in vitro transcription with T7 RNA polymerase using a HiScribe™ T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB).

Reconstituted human cell-free translation

Reconstituted cell-free translation was carried out as described61 with modifications. Briefly, 1.26 mg/mL eEF1 complex, 100 μg/mL eEF2, 5 μg/mL eRF1/3, 0.48 μM 40S ribosomal subunit, 0.48 μM 60S ribosomal subunit, 150 μg/mL aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase mixture, and 100 μM amino acid mixture (Promega) were mixed in a test tube in reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 55.2 mM potassium acetate, 6 mM magnesium acetate, 200 μM spermidine trihydrochloride, 60 μg/mL creatine kinase, 20.6 mM creatine phosphate, 1 mM DTT, 1.36 mM ATP, and 840 μM each of GTP, UTP, and CTP. This reaction mixture was then mixed with mRNA (1.56 μM Rluc mRNA, or 1.66 μM mHiBiT mRNA) and total tRNAs with or without desulfuration (1 mg/mL) in a final volume of 10 μL, followed by incubation at 32 °C for 3 h.

Luciferase assay

One microliter of cell-free translation product of Rluc or mHiBiT mRNAs was used for measuring luciferase activity using a Renilla Luciferase Assay System (Promega) or a Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic Detection System (Promega), respectively. Relative light units (RLU) of luminescence were measured by a GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega).

Polysomal RNA preparation

HEK293T cells (~80% confluency) cultured in 25 dishes with 15-cm diameter were lysed with a lysis buffer comprising 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, 50 µg/mL cycloheximide. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell lysate was loaded onto a cushion buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 µg/ml cycloheximide, and 20% sucrose] and ultracentrifuged at 35,000 rpm and 4 °C for 4 h by a Optima L-90K ultracentrifuge (Beckman) with a 70 Ti rotor (Beckman). Free RNA fraction was extracted from the supernatant using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Roche). The crude ribosome pellet was re-suspended in a ribosome binding buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 µg/ml cycloheximide], and then loaded onto a 10-50% sucrose gradient in an SDG buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 µg/ml cycloheximide] and ultracentrifuged at 25,000 rpm and 4 °C for 4.5 h by a Optima L-90K ultracentrifuge (Beckman) with a SW28 rotor (Beckman). The gradients were fractionated with continuous measurement of absorbance at 260 nm using a TRIAX flow cell (BioComp) and a fraction collector (Gilson, Fisher Scientific). The polysomal fraction was collected, followed by RNA precipitation by adding 3 volume of ethanol and incubating overnight at –80 °C85. Then, polysomal RNA was extracted from the pellet using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Roche). To concentrate small RNA fraction, polysomal RNA, free RNA, and total RNA were subjected to an anion-exchange resin (DEAE Sepharose Fast Flow; GE Healthcare) with low salt buffer [10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT]. After washing the resin with the same buffer, small RNA fractions were eluted with high salt buffer [10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl and 2 mM DTT], followed by ethanol precipitation.

Aminoacylation assay

Aminoacylation of each tRNA was performed in a 20 μL reaction mixture containing 100 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 50 mM KCl, 6 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 2 mM ATP, 15–30 μM 14C-labeled amino acids, 20 μM total tRNA with or without desulfuration and 750 ng aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase mixture61 at 37 °C. Before aminoacylation reaction, total tRNA was deacylated with 100 mM CHES-NaOH (pH 9.0) at 37 °C for 45 min. The concentration and specific activity of 14C-labeled amino acids used in this study were as follows: 15 μM L-[14C(U)]-Threonine (6.5 GBq/mmol; American Radiolabeled Chemicals), 15 μM L-[14C(U)]-Arginine (11.1 GBq/mmol; American Radiolabeled Chemicals), 30 μM L-[14C(U)]-Glutamine (9.0 GBq/mmol; PerkinElmer), 30 μM L-[14C(U)]- Glutamic acid (8.1 GBq/mmol; American Radiolabeled Chemicals), and 15 μM L-[14C(U)]-Lysine (12.1 GBq/mmol; PerkinElmer). At each time point, the reaction was quenched by spotting 4 μL aliquots of reaction mixture onto a Whatman 3MM Chr filter paper (Cytiva). The filter paper was pre-soaked in a 5% trichloroacetic acid solution before use. The spotted filter paper was washed three times for 15 min each in ice-cold 5% trichloroacetic acid, washed with ethanol and dried under a heat lamp. Acid-insoluble radioactivity of aminoacyl-tRNAs was measured by a liquid scintillation analyzer (PerkinElmer).

Ribosomal A-site tRNA binding

tRNA binding to the A-site on the ribosome was carried out as described86,87 with modifications. mRNAs containing the Shine-Dalgarno sequence and an AAR codon (R=A or G) codons or a UUC codon (negative control) at the A-site (Supplementary Data 6) were chemically synthesized. Human cytoplasmic tRNALys bearing mcm5s2U or mcm5h2U was dephosphorylated in buffer containing 10 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5) and 0.008 units of bacterial alkaline phosphatase (E. coli C75; Takara) at 55 °C for 30 min, followed by gel purification of the intact tRNAs. The dephosphorylated tRNAs were 5′ phosphorylated with [γ−32P]ATP (PerkinElmer) by T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB), followed by gel purification of the 5′−32P-labeled tRNAs. Radioactivity of labeled tRNAs was measured by Cherenkov counting using a liquid scintillation analyzer (PerkinElmer). The specific activities of labeled tRNAs were adjusted to 20,000 cpm/pmol by adding respective cold tRNALys with or without desulfuration. First, E. coli 70S ribosomes (1.6 pmol) were incubated with E. coli tRNAfMet (25 pmol) and mRNA (20 pmol) at 37 °C for 40 min in binding buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 120 mM KCl, 6.5 mM Mg(OAc)2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.5 mM spermine to occupy the P-site of the ribosome. Next, 0.1, 0.25, or 0.5 pmol of the 32P-labeled tRNALys was added to the mixture and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, then at 0 °C for 30 min. The mixture was spotted onto double-layered Amersham Protran nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva) and Hybond-N+ nylon membrane (Cytiva) using a 96-well dot-blot vacuum apparatus (SANPLATEC, Japan). The membranes were washed twice with 200 μL of binding buffer, dried, exposed to an imaging plate, and visualized on an FLA-7000 image analyzer (Fujifilm). The amount of bound tRNA was quantified using Multi Gauge V3.0 (Fujifilm).

Single-particle cryo-EM analysis of ribosomes with human tRNAs

The cryo-EM single-particle analysis of the ribosome–tRNA complexes was basically performed in accordance with our previous studies5,78,86,88. E. coli 70S ribosomes were prepared from the MRE600 strain as previously described5, resuspended in RBS buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), 10 mM Mg(OAc)2, 30 mM NH4Cl, and 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Methionylation and formylation of tRNAiMet were carried out as described previously89. To form a ribosome complex, E. coli 70S ribosomes were mixed with mRNA and P-site tRNA in a mixture containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), 10 mM Mg(OAc)2, 30 mM NH4Cl, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 nM 70S ribosomes, 500 nM mRNA, and 500 nM E. coli fMet-tRNAiMet, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Next, 500 nM of human tRNALys was added as A-site tRNA and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by further incubation on ice for 1 h. Grid preparation was carried out as previously described5.

Ribosome particle images were collected using a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV and equipped with a K3 camera (Gatan). Automated image acquisition was performed at a nominal magnification of ×105,000 with a defocus range from 0.5 to 2.5 mm using a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) in CDS-counting mode (0.8285 Å/pixel; Supplementary Data 3). The total exposure of electrons at the specimen level was ~50 e−/Å2, and dose was fractionated into 48 frames (Supplementary Data 3).

Images were recoded as movie micrographs and image processing was performed using RELION-3.1.390. Details of image processing for each ribosome complex are described in Supplementary Fig. 5. Movies were motion-corrected with the program implemented in RELION, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with CTFFIND-4.191. Ribosome particles were automatically picked by crYOLO-1.8.292. The obtained particles were initially subjected to 2D classification to discard ice contamination, and the remaining particles were then auto-refined to reconstruct a consensus map. Subsequently, based on the alignment of consensus reconstruction, 3D classification was performed. The subgroup of 70S ribosome particles was auto-refined and further classified using a mask around A-site tRNA to discard ribosomes with a vacant A-site. Subgroups of A-site tRNA-bound ribosomes were further auto-refined followed by CTF refinement, Bayesian polishing and another round of 3D refinement and postprocessing. The final resolution reached 2.26–3.18 Å. The angular distribution of particle projections was calculated by RELION-3.1.390. The local resolution of each map was estimated by RELION-3.1.390 (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d).

The high-resolution structure of the E. coli 70S ribosome complexed with P-site tRNAiMet and A-site tRNA (PDB: 7y7e)5 was used as a starting model and ridged body-fitted to the obtained cryo-EM maps. Models of mRNA and A-site tRNA were manually constructed using COOT-0.9.593, and tRNA modifications were manually inserted into the model. The chemical structure of mcm5h2U was modeled using eLBOW94. These initial models were real space-refined to cryo-EM maps by the phenix.real space refine routine95. These models were corrected using UCSF Chimera-1.1496 and COOT-0.9.593, followed by a final phenix.real space refine routine using Phenix-1.1895. Refined final models were validated using MOLPROBITY97. Map vs. model FSC values were calculated using Phenix-1.1895 (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Graphical figures were prepared using UCSF Chimera-1.1496 or UCSF ChimeraX 1.3 98.

Synonymous codon usage bias analysis

Gene lists associated with the Gene Ontology (GO) term “response to oxidative stress” (GO:0006979) were retrieved using AmiGO 2 (http://amigo.geneontology.org). Gene names were mapped to corresponding NCBI RefSeq accessions using the UniProt ID mapping tool (https://www.uniprot.org/id-mapping). The coding sequences (CDS) corresponding to the RefSeq identifiers were then downloaded from NCBI in FASTA format. For each sequence, valid open reading frames (ORFs)—defined as regions starting with a canonical ATG initiation codon and terminating at the first in-frame stop codon (TAA, TAG, or TGA)—were extracted from the FASTA files.

Cryo-EM maps and atomic coordinates of the reported structures were deposited in the Electron Microscopy Data Bank (EMDB) and Protein Data Bank, respectively, with the following accession codes: EMD-61706 and 9JPM (tRNALys with mcm5s2U34 and the AAA codon); EMD-61708 and 9JPO (tRNALys with mcm5h2U34 and the AAA codon); EMD-61709 and 9JPP (tRNALys with mcm5s2U34 and the AAG codon); EMD-61720 and 9JQ2 (tRNALys with mcm5h2U34 and the AAG codon). Source data are provided with this paper.

The code generated in this study has been deposited in Zenodo under 10.5281/zenodo.18289446.
